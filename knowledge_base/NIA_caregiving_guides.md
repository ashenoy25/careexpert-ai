## 2. Being a Caregiver

**Source URL**: https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/caregiving/being-caregiver
**Title**: Being a Caregiver
**Key Topics**: Caregiver roles, responsibilities, emotional impact, self-care, getting started, types of help

### What Is a Caregiver?

A caregiver is anyone who helps care for a person with a health condition or disability. Caregivers may be family members, friends, or neighbors. They may provide help with daily tasks such as bathing, dressing, managing medications, preparing meals, or providing transportation to medical appointments.

Caregiving can be a rewarding experience, but it is also demanding. Understanding your role and knowing where to find help are important first steps.

### Who Are Caregivers?

- More than 53 million Americans serve as unpaid caregivers
- The majority of caregivers are women, though men also play significant caregiving roles
- Caregivers come from all walks of life and age groups
- Some are caring for a spouse, parent, sibling, friend, or neighbor
- Many caregivers also work and have families of their own

### Getting Started as a Caregiver

When someone you know needs care, it can feel overwhelming. NIA recommends the following steps:

1. **Learn about the condition**: Understanding the diagnosis helps you anticipate needs and plan ahead
2. **Talk to the health care team**: Ask questions about the condition, treatments, and what to expect
3. **Find out what kind of help is needed**: Assess daily living needs -- bathing, dressing, eating, mobility, medication management, transportation
4. **Organize important documents**: Gather medical records, insurance information, legal documents (advance directives, power of attorney)
5. **Build a support network**: Identify other family members, friends, or community services that can help
6. **Take care of yourself**: Caregiver health is critical; schedule your own medical appointments and find time for rest

### Types of Care You May Provide

- **Personal care**: Bathing, dressing, grooming, toileting
- **Household tasks**: Cooking, cleaning, laundry, home maintenance
- **Health care tasks**: Managing medications, wound care, operating medical equipment, communicating with health providers
- **Emotional support**: Companionship, conversation, encouragement, managing behavioral changes
- **Care coordination**: Scheduling appointments, managing insurance, coordinating among multiple providers
- **Financial management**: Paying bills, managing accounts, handling insurance claims

### Emotional Aspects of Caregiving

Caregiving can bring a wide range of emotions:

- **Satisfaction and purpose**: Many caregivers find meaning in helping a loved one
- **Stress and anxiety**: The demands of caregiving can be physically and emotionally exhausting
- **Guilt**: Caregivers may feel guilty about not doing enough, or about having negative feelings
- **Grief and loss**: Watching a loved one's health decline can trigger grief, even before death
- **Anger and frustration**: These are normal reactions to difficult caregiving situations
- **Isolation**: Caregivers may withdraw from social activities and feel lonely

### Caregiver Self-Care Tips

- Make time for yourself every day, even if it is brief
- Stay physically active
- Eat well and get enough sleep
- Accept help from others -- you do not have to do everything alone
- Join a caregiver support group (in person or online)
- Talk to a counselor or therapist if you feel overwhelmed
- Stay connected with friends and family
- Set realistic goals and expectations
- Recognize your limits
- Use respite care services to take breaks

### When to Ask for Help

Signs you may need more support:

- Feeling overwhelmed or constantly exhausted
- Sleeping too much or too little
- Gaining or losing weight
- Getting sick more often
- Feeling increasingly irritable or anxious
- Losing interest in activities you used to enjoy
- Feeling sad or hopeless
- Misusing alcohol, drugs, or medications

## 3. Alzheimer's and Dementia Caregiving

**Source URL**: https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-and-dementia/caregiving
**Title**: Caregiving for Someone with Alzheimer's Disease or a Related Dementia
**Key Topics**: Dementia-specific caregiving, behavioral symptoms, communication, daily care, late-stage care, caregiver support

### Overview

Caring for a person with Alzheimer's disease or a related dementia presents unique challenges. As the disease progresses, the person will need increasing levels of care and supervision. NIA provides guidance across the full spectrum of dementia caregiving.

### Topics and Subtopics Covered

#### Communication
- **Communicating with a person with dementia**: Use simple sentences, speak slowly and clearly, give one-step directions, use visual cues and gestures, be patient and allow time for response, avoid arguing or correcting
- As the disease progresses, rely more on nonverbal communication -- tone of voice, facial expressions, touch

#### Daily Care and Activities
- **Bathing**: Establish a routine, prepare everything in advance, use a handheld showerhead, ensure bathroom safety (grab bars, nonslip mats), be gentle and respect dignity
- **Dressing**: Lay out clothing in order, choose comfortable clothes with simple fasteners (Velcro, elastic waistbands), allow the person to do as much as possible independently
- **Eating and nutrition**: Offer familiar foods, serve one food at a time, use adaptive utensils, ensure adequate hydration, monitor for swallowing difficulties
- **Activities and engagement**: Adapt activities to the person's abilities, focus on enjoyment rather than achievement, try music, art, gardening, simple household tasks, reminiscence activities
- **Exercise and physical activity**: Walking, light stretching, seated exercises; physical activity can improve mood, maintain mobility, and promote better sleep
- **Incontinence**: Establish regular toileting schedules, use signs or pictures to mark the bathroom, choose easy-to-remove clothing, consult a doctor about underlying causes

#### Managing Behavioral Symptoms
- **Agitation and aggression**: Stay calm, try to identify triggers (pain, overstimulation, fatigue, frustration), redirect attention, simplify the environment
- **Anxiety and sundowning**: Sundowning refers to increased confusion and agitation in the late afternoon and evening; maintain a consistent routine, reduce stimulation in the evening, ensure good lighting, limit caffeine
- **Wandering**: Use door alarms, ID bracelets (such as MedicAlert + Safe Return), keep a recent photo available, notify neighbors, secure the home
- **Sleep problems**: Encourage physical activity during the day, limit daytime napping, maintain a consistent bedtime routine, reduce evening stimulation, ensure a comfortable sleeping environment
- **Hallucinations and delusions**: Do not argue with the person; offer reassurance, redirect attention, check for environmental causes (shadows, reflections), consult the health care team
- **Repetitive behaviors**: Respond calmly, provide reassurance, redirect with an activity or snack, avoid reminding the person they already asked

#### Caregiver Health and Coping
- Dementia caregiving is associated with higher rates of depression, anxiety, and physical health problems
- Caregivers should monitor their own health, seek respite, attend support groups, and consider counseling
- Respite care options include in-home aides, adult day care programs, and short-term residential care

#### Late-Stage and End-of-Life Care
- In the final stages of dementia, care focuses on comfort and quality of life
- Common issues include difficulty eating and swallowing, immobility, infections (particularly pneumonia and urinary tract infections), skin breakdown
- Hospice and palliative care can provide specialized support
- Advance care planning conversations should happen early in the disease process

#### Safety
- **Home safety**: Remove tripping hazards, install locks and safety devices, secure medications and chemicals, monitor cooking and driving ability
- **Driving**: People with dementia eventually become unable to drive safely; this is a difficult but critical conversation
- **Wandering prevention**: Secure exits, use monitoring technology, enroll in safe-return programs

### Key Resources for Dementia Caregivers

- **ALZConnected online community**: alzconnected.org
- **Caregiver Action Network**: caregiveraction.org

## 4. Falls and Falls Prevention

**Source URL**: https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/falls-and-falls-prevention
**Title**: Falls and Falls Prevention
**Key Topics**: Fall risk factors, prevention strategies, exercise, home safety, medications review, vision and foot care

### Overview

Falls are a leading cause of injury among older adults. Each year, millions of older people (those 65 and older) fall. One out of five falls causes a serious injury, such as a broken bone or head injury. Falls can also lead to fear of falling, reduced physical activity, loss of independence, and even death. The good news is that falls are not an inevitable part of aging and many falls can be prevented.

### Why Do Older Adults Fall?

Falls result from a combination of risk factors. The more risk factors a person has, the greater the chance of falling.

#### Biological/Health Risk Factors
- **Lower body weakness**: Weak legs increase the risk of falls
- **Balance and gait problems**: Difficulty with walking and balance
- **Vision problems**: Poor vision, cataracts, glaucoma, macular degeneration
- **Foot pain or poor footwear**: Foot problems and inappropriate shoes
- **Chronic conditions**: Arthritis, diabetes, stroke, Parkinson's disease, dementia, heart disease
- **Vitamin D deficiency**: Low vitamin D may contribute to muscle weakness
- **Medications**: Certain drugs (sedatives, antidepressants, blood pressure medications) can cause dizziness, drowsiness, or low blood pressure
- **Postural hypotension**: A drop in blood pressure when standing up

#### Environmental Risk Factors
- Broken or uneven steps or walkways
- Loose rugs or clutter on floors
- Poor lighting, especially on stairs
- No handrails or grab bars
- Wet or slippery surfaces

### How to Prevent Falls

NIA recommends a multi-faceted approach to fall prevention:

#### 1. Talk to Your Health Care Provider
- Ask about your personal fall risk
- Review all medications (prescription, over-the-counter, supplements) -- some may increase fall risk through side effects like dizziness or drowsiness
- Discuss any previous falls, even if you were not injured
- Ask about vitamin D supplementation
- Have your blood pressure checked lying down and standing up

#### 2. Exercise to Improve Strength and Balance
- Regular physical activity is one of the most effective ways to reduce fall risk
- Focus on exercises that improve leg strength, balance, and flexibility
- Examples: Tai Chi, walking, water aerobics, resistance training, yoga
- The CDC's STEADI program and evidence-based programs like A Matter of Balance, Tai Chi: Moving for Better Balance, and Stepping On are recommended
- Start slowly and build up gradually; consult your health care provider before starting

#### 3. Have Your Eyes and Feet Checked
- Get your eyes checked at least once a year; update eyeglasses as needed
- If you have bifocals or progressive lenses, use caution on stairs (the bottom portion can make steps appear blurry)
- Have your feet checked regularly; discuss foot pain, numbness, or tingling with your provider
- Wear well-fitting, sturdy shoes with nonskid soles; avoid walking in socks, stockings, or slippers without traction

#### 4. Make Your Home Safer
- **Remove tripping hazards**: Pick up clutter, secure loose rugs with double-sided tape or remove them, keep walkways clear
- **Add grab bars and handrails**: In the bathroom (near the toilet and in the shower/tub) and on both sides of stairways
- **Improve lighting**: Use bright lighting throughout the home, especially on stairs and in hallways; add night lights in bedrooms, bathrooms, and hallways
- **Organize your home**: Keep items you use often within easy reach; avoid using step stools
- **Use assistive devices as needed**: Canes, walkers, or other mobility aids recommended by a health care provider
- **Install nonslip mats**: In the bathtub/shower and on bathroom floors
- **Ensure stairs are safe**: Have handrails on both sides, ensure steps are even and in good repair, add nonslip treads

#### 5. Additional Prevention Strategies
- Get up slowly from sitting or lying down to prevent dizziness
- Use a cane or walker if you feel unsteady
- Wear sensible shoes both inside and outside the home
- Be cautious on wet or icy surfaces
- Use good lighting when moving around at night
- Consider a personal emergency response system (medical alert device)

### What to Do If You Fall

- Try to stay calm
- Assess whether you are injured before getting up
- If you can get up safely: Roll onto your side, push up to a hands-and-knees position, crawl to a sturdy piece of furniture, use it to support yourself as you stand
- If you cannot get up: Call for help; use a phone, medical alert device, or bang on the floor/wall to attract attention; try to stay warm and move as best you can while waiting for help
- Always tell your health care provider about a fall, even if you are not injured

### Fall-Related Statistics

- Each year about 36 million falls are reported among older adults
- Falls result in more than 32,000 deaths per year among older adults
- About 3 million older adults are treated in emergency departments for fall injuries annually
- Over 800,000 patients are hospitalized because of fall injuries, most often for head injuries or hip fractures
- Falls are the most common cause of traumatic brain injuries (TBI) in older adults
- The cost of falls in older adults was approximately $50 billion in medical costs annually

## 5. Home Safety and Alzheimer's Disease

**Source URL**: https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/safety/home-safety-and-alzheimers-disease
**Title**: Home Safety and Alzheimer's Disease
**Key Topics**: Room-by-room safety checklist, wandering prevention, adapting the home environment, emergency preparedness

### Overview

Making a home safe for a person with Alzheimer's disease or a related dementia is essential. People with dementia gradually lose the ability to judge and navigate hazards. Adapting the home environment can help prevent injuries and reduce caregiver stress. Home safety should be reassessed regularly as the disease progresses.

### General Home Safety Principles

- **Think prevention**: Look at the home through the eyes of someone with dementia -- identify and address potential hazards before accidents occur
- **Simplify the environment**: Remove unnecessary items, reduce clutter, minimize distractions
- **Adapt as needed**: As abilities decline, make ongoing modifications to the home
- **Supervise appropriately**: The level of supervision needed will increase as the disease progresses
- **Use locks and safety devices**: Install locks, alarms, and safety devices as appropriate

### Room-by-Room Safety Checklist

#### Kitchen
- Install safety knobs or disconnect the stove when not in use; consider an automatic shut-off device
- Remove or lock up sharp knives, scissors, and other dangerous utensils
- Store household chemicals, cleaning products, and matches/lighters in a locked cabinet
- Remove or disable the garbage disposal
- Keep a working fire extinguisher in the kitchen
- Remove refrigerator magnets and small items that could be swallowed
- Use unbreakable dishes and cups
- Remove artificial fruits and food-shaped decorations (the person may try to eat them)
- Adjust the hot water heater to 120 degrees Fahrenheit or lower to prevent scalding
- Install childproof latches on cabinets containing hazardous items
- Supervise the person in the kitchen; do not leave someone with dementia alone while cooking

#### Bathroom
- Install grab bars near the toilet and in the shower/tub
- Use a raised toilet seat if needed
- Place nonslip strips or mats in the bathtub/shower and on the floor
- Remove or lock up medications, razors, scissors, and cleaning supplies
- Use a handheld showerhead for easier bathing
- Consider a shower chair or bath bench
- Set the water heater to 120 degrees Fahrenheit or lower
- Remove electrical appliances (hair dryers, curling irons) from the bathroom or lock them away
- Never leave the person alone in the bathroom
- Remove the lock on the bathroom door (or be able to open it from the outside) to prevent the person from locking themselves in
- Use a nightlight for nighttime use

#### Bedroom
- Use a night light or low-wattage nightlight
- Use a bed at an appropriate height; consider a hospital bed or bed rail if there is risk of falling out of bed (note: bed rails carry entrapment risks; discuss with the health care team)
- Remove clutter and tripping hazards
- Place a monitor (baby monitor or intercom) in the room to listen for problems
- Keep a clear path from the bed to the bathroom
- Remove portable heaters
- Install smoke detectors

#### Living Areas and General Spaces
- Remove or secure loose rugs and runners; use nonskid backing
- Arrange furniture to create clear walking paths
- Remove or pad sharp corners on furniture
- Keep electrical cords out of walkways
- Cover unused electrical outlets with safety plugs
- Remove or secure fragile items and valuables
- Ensure adequate lighting -- add lamps and nightlights as needed
- Lock up or remove firearms, power tools, and other dangerous items
- Install smoke detectors and carbon monoxide detectors on every level
- Keep a well-stocked first aid kit accessible
- Remove or secure anything that could be mistaken for food (potpourri, decorative soaps, candles)
- Consider removing or covering mirrors if they cause confusion or agitation

#### Stairs and Hallways
- Install handrails on both sides of stairways
- Ensure stairs are well-lit
- Apply nonslip treads to steps
- Place a gate at the top and bottom of stairs if the person has difficulty with stairs
- Remove clutter from hallways
- Use contrasting tape on the edges of steps to make them more visible

#### Outdoors and Garage
- Keep walkways clear and in good repair
- Ensure outdoor lighting is adequate
- Lock the garage and any outbuildings
- Remove or lock up tools, chemicals, fertilizers, and gasoline
- Secure the yard with fencing if the person tends to wander
- Remove poisonous plants
- Cover or secure swimming pools and hot tubs
- Lock gates and install alarms on doors leading outside

### Preventing Wandering

Wandering is a common and dangerous behavior in people with Alzheimer's disease. Strategies include:

- **Door and window alarms**: Install alarms that sound when doors or windows are opened
- **Door locks**: Use deadbolts that require a key on both sides or place locks up high or down low where they are less visible (check local fire codes)
- **Camouflage exits**: Place a curtain or poster over doors; paint doors to match surrounding walls; place a dark mat or dark tape in front of doors (people with Alzheimer's may perceive dark areas as holes and avoid stepping on them)
- **Identification**: The person should carry or wear identification at all times; enroll in MedicAlert + Alzheimer's Association Safe Return program or a similar tracking service
- **Notify others**: Let neighbors and local police know that the person may wander
- **Keep a recent photo**: Have a current, clear photo readily available in case the person goes missing
- **GPS tracking devices**: Consider using a GPS tracking device (wearable or placed in clothing/shoes)
- **Secure the home**: Check that all exits are secured, especially at night
- **Monitor patterns**: Learn when wandering is most likely and plan supervision accordingly

### Emergency Preparedness

- Keep a list of emergency phone numbers in a visible location
- Register with local emergency services and provide information about the person's condition
- Prepare a disaster supply kit that includes medications, medical information, comfort items, and extra clothing
- Have an evacuation plan and practice it
- Consider enrolling in a community alert program

### Adapting Over Time

Home safety needs change as Alzheimer's disease progresses:

- **Early stage**: The person may still be relatively independent but may forget to turn off appliances, get lost in familiar places, or have difficulty managing medications. Focus on: removing obvious hazards, organizing medications, establishing routines, updating legal and financial documents.

- **Middle stage**: The person needs more help with daily activities and supervision increases. Focus on: bathroom safety modifications, kitchen safety, wandering prevention, simplifying the environment, considering adult day programs for structured activities and caregiver respite.

- **Late stage**: The person is largely dependent on others for care. Focus on: bed safety, skin care to prevent pressure sores, safe feeding and swallowing, preventing infections, comfort measures, ensuring 24-hour supervision.

## Cross-Cutting Themes Across All NIA Caregiving Resources

### 1. Person-Centered Care
- Respect the dignity and preferences of the person receiving care
- Involve the person in decisions as much as possible
- Adapt care approaches as needs change

### 2. Caregiver Well-Being
- Caregiver health is consistently emphasized across all resources
- Self-care is not selfish -- it is essential for sustainable caregiving
- Support groups, respite care, and counseling are strongly recommended

### 3. Safety and Prevention
- Proactive hazard identification prevents injuries
- Regular reassessment of safety needs as conditions progress
- Multi-component approaches (exercise, home modification, medication review) are most effective

### 4. Communication and Care Coordination
- Effective communication with health care providers
- Building a care team (family, friends, professionals)
- Planning ahead for changing needs (advance directives, legal planning)

### 5. Evidence-Based Approaches
- NIA recommendations are grounded in clinical research
- Exercise programs, fall prevention strategies, and home modifications are supported by evidence
- Referrals to specific programs (STEADI, Tai Chi, Safe Return) reflect evidence-based practice
