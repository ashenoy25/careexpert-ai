## 1. Core Infection Prevention and Control Practices for Healthcare

**Source:** CDC - Core Infection Prevention and Control Practices for Safe Healthcare Delivery in All Settings
**URL:** https://www.cdc.gov/infection-control/hcp/core-practices/index.html
**Last Known Update:** 2024 (revised from 2017 guidance)

### 1.1 Overview

The CDC Core Practices provide a foundation of infection prevention and control (IPC) recommendations that apply across all healthcare settings, including home health, ambulatory care, hospitals, and long-term care facilities. They represent the minimum infection prevention expectations for safe care delivery.

### 1.2 Core Practice Categories

#### A. Hand Hygiene
- Perform hand hygiene before and after every patient contact
- Use alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) with 60-95% alcohol as the preferred method when hands are not visibly soiled
- Use soap and water when hands are visibly soiled, after caring for patients with C. difficile, or after using the restroom
- Perform hand hygiene before donning and after doffing personal protective equipment (PPE)

#### B. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)
- Select PPE based on the anticipated exposure and route of transmission
- **Gloves:** Wear when anticipating contact with blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, non-intact skin, or contaminated equipment
- **Gowns/Isolation gowns:** Wear to protect skin and clothing during procedures likely to generate splashes or sprays
- **Face protection (masks, goggles, face shields):** Wear during procedures that may generate splashes or sprays of blood, body fluids, secretions, or excretions
- **Respirators (N95 or higher):** Wear when caring for patients with suspected or confirmed airborne infections
- Remove PPE carefully to avoid self-contamination; perform hand hygiene after removal

#### C. Injection Safety and Medication Handling
- Use aseptic technique when preparing and administering injections
- Never reuse needles, syringes, or single-dose vials for more than one patient
- Use single-dose vials whenever possible
- If multi-dose vials must be used, dedicate them to a single patient whenever possible
- Do not store medications in immediate patient care areas unless required

#### D. Respiratory Hygiene / Cough Etiquette
- Cover mouth and nose when coughing or sneezing with tissue or elbow
- Dispose of tissues in waste receptacle
- Perform hand hygiene after contact with respiratory secretions
- Offer surgical masks to symptomatic persons upon entry to healthcare facilities
- Spatial separation of at least 3-6 feet from symptomatic individuals when possible

#### E. Environmental Cleaning and Disinfection
- Establish and follow regular schedules for cleaning and disinfection of environmental surfaces
- Clean and disinfect high-touch surfaces more frequently
- Use EPA-registered hospital-grade disinfectants according to manufacturer instructions
- Clean visibly soiled surfaces before disinfection
- Maintain cleaning and disinfection procedures for shared medical equipment between patients

#### F. Reprocessing of Reusable Medical Devices
- Clean, disinfect, or sterilize reusable medical devices according to manufacturer instructions
- Categorize devices by Spaulding classification (critical, semi-critical, non-critical)
- Follow proper protocols for each level of reprocessing
- Verify sterilization processes through monitoring programs

### 1.3 Key Principles for All Settings (Including Home Health)

1. **Standard Precautions** apply to all patient care regardless of suspected or confirmed infection status
2. **Transmission-Based Precautions** are used in addition to Standard Precautions for patients with documented or suspected infections
3. Healthcare personnel (HCP) education and training on IPC is essential
4. Competency demonstration should be ongoing, not limited to orientation
5. Facility-level IPC programs should include surveillance, reporting, and performance improvement
6. Source control measures should be implemented for patients with respiratory symptoms

### 1.4 Key Recommendations for Caregivers in Home Settings

- Apply Standard Precautions during all patient interactions
- Ensure hand hygiene supplies are readily available
- Use proper PPE when exposure to blood or body fluids is anticipated
- Maintain a clean care environment
- Properly handle and dispose of sharps and contaminated waste
- Report infections and follow protocols for notification
- Stay current with recommended immunizations

## 2. STEADI - Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths & Injuries (Fall Prevention)

**Source:** CDC STEADI Initiative - Older Adult Fall Prevention
**URL:** https://www.cdc.gov/steadi/index.html
**Last Known Update:** 2024

### 2.1 Overview

STEADI (Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths & Injuries) is the CDC's comprehensive initiative for healthcare providers to screen, assess, and intervene to reduce fall risk among older adults (age 65+). Falls are the leading cause of injury and injury-related death among adults aged 65 and older.

### 2.2 Fall Statistics (CDC Data)

- Approximately 1 in 4 adults aged 65+ falls each year
- Falls result in approximately 3 million emergency department visits annually
- Over 36,000 older adults die from fall-related injuries each year
- Falls are the most common cause of traumatic brain injuries (TBI)
- The total medical cost for falls exceeds $50 billion annually

### 2.3 The STEADI Algorithm: Screen, Assess, Intervene

#### Step 1: SCREEN for Fall Risk
Three key screening questions (any "yes" indicates risk):
1. Have you fallen in the past year?
2. Do you feel unsteady when standing or walking?
3. Do you worry about falling?

**Stay Independent Questionnaire** - a 12-item self-assessment tool for patients:
- Score of 4 or higher indicates increased fall risk
- Covers questions about balance, steadiness, use of assistive devices, medication effects

#### Step 2: ASSESS Modifiable Risk Factors
Conduct a multifactorial assessment including:

**A. Gait, Strength, and Balance Assessment**
- **Timed Up and Go (TUG) Test:** Patient stands from chair, walks 10 feet, turns, walks back, sits. 12 seconds or more suggests fall risk
- **30-Second Chair Stand Test:** Counts the number of times a patient can stand from a seated position in 30 seconds. Below-average scores indicate fall risk
- **4-Stage Balance Test:** Assesses ability to hold progressively challenging positions (feet together, semi-tandem, tandem, single-leg stand) for 10 seconds each. Inability to hold tandem for 10 seconds suggests fall risk

**B. Medication Review**
- Review all medications (prescription, OTC, supplements)
- Identify high-risk medications: sedatives, antidepressants, antihypertensives, opioids, anticholinergics
- Use the STEADI medication review checklist
- Reduce or discontinue high-risk medications when clinically appropriate
- Focus on polypharmacy (4+ medications)

**C. Orthostatic Hypotension Evaluation**
- Measure blood pressure and heart rate in supine and standing positions
- A drop of 20+ mmHg systolic or 10+ mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing indicates orthostatic hypotension
- Identify and address contributing medications

**D. Vision Assessment**
- Assess visual acuity
- Refer for comprehensive eye examination if indicated
- Address corrective lens needs
- Evaluate impact of cataracts, glaucoma, macular degeneration

**E. Feet and Footwear Assessment**
- Assess for foot pain, deformities, and peripheral neuropathy
- Evaluate footwear for proper fit, support, and non-skid soles
- Refer to podiatry when indicated

**F. Home Safety Evaluation**
- Assess for environmental hazards: loose rugs, poor lighting, clutter, lack of grab bars
- Use the CDC Home Fall Prevention Checklist
- Recommend modifications to reduce risk

#### Step 3: INTERVENE to Reduce Risk

**A. Exercise and Physical Therapy**
- Recommend evidence-based exercise programs focusing on balance, strength, and gait
- Examples: Tai Chi, Otago Exercise Programme, Stepping On
- Minimum recommendation: moderate-intensity exercise 150 minutes/week
- Include balance training at least 3 days per week

**B. Medication Management**
- Optimize medications to reduce fall risk
- Taper or discontinue high-risk medications where possible
- Minimize polypharmacy

**C. Vitamin D Supplementation**
- Recommend vitamin D supplementation (typically 800-1000 IU/day) for adults with vitamin D deficiency or insufficiency
- Note: USPSTF updated recommendations; individualized assessment is recommended

**D. Home Modifications**
- Install grab bars in bathrooms (near toilet and in shower/tub)
- Improve lighting throughout the home, especially stairways and hallways
- Remove tripping hazards (loose rugs, clutter, cords)
- Install handrails on both sides of stairways
- Use non-slip mats in bathrooms
- Ensure adequate contrast on stair edges

**E. Referrals**
- Physical therapy for gait and balance training
- Occupational therapy for home safety assessment and ADL training
- Ophthalmology/optometry for vision concerns
- Podiatry for foot problems
- Pharmacy for medication review

### 2.4 STEADI Resources and Tools

- STEADI Algorithm (clinical workflow)
- Stay Independent Brochure (patient self-assessment)
- Check for Safety Brochure (home safety checklist)
- Talking to Your Doctor About Falls (patient guide)
- Case Studies for clinical training
- Conversation starters for healthcare providers

### 2.5 Relevance to Home Health Caregiving

- Home health aides are often the first to observe fall risk factors
- Caregivers should be trained to identify environmental hazards
- Report changes in gait, balance, or mobility to the supervising nurse
- Assist patients with prescribed exercise programs
- Ensure assistive devices are properly used and maintained
- Monitor for medication side effects that increase fall risk

## 3. Hand Hygiene in Healthcare Settings

**Source:** CDC - About Hand Hygiene in Healthcare Settings
**URL:** https://www.cdc.gov/hand-hygiene/about/index.html
**Last Known Update:** 2024

### 3.1 Overview

Hand hygiene is the single most important practice to prevent the transmission of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs). The CDC provides comprehensive guidance on when and how to perform hand hygiene in all healthcare settings, including home health care.

### 3.2 When to Perform Hand Hygiene (The "5 Moments")

Based on the WHO "My 5 Moments for Hand Hygiene" framework, adapted by CDC:

1. **Before touching a patient** - to protect the patient from harmful germs on your hands
2. **Before a clean/aseptic procedure** - to prevent introducing germs to a susceptible site
3. **After body fluid exposure risk** - to protect yourself and the healthcare environment
4. **After touching a patient** - to protect yourself and the healthcare environment
5. **After touching patient surroundings** - to protect yourself and the healthcare environment

### 3.3 Additional Situations Requiring Hand Hygiene

- Before and after eating
- After using the restroom
- After sneezing, coughing, or blowing your nose
- Before and after donning/doffing gloves
- When moving from a contaminated body site to a clean body site during patient care
- After touching surfaces or objects in the patient's environment
- Before preparing or handling medications or food

### 3.4 Methods of Hand Hygiene

#### A. Alcohol-Based Hand Rub (ABHR) - Preferred Method
**When to use:** When hands are not visibly soiled

**Technique:**
1. Apply product to palm of one hand (amount per manufacturer recommendation)
2. Rub hands together covering all surfaces of hands and fingers
3. Continue rubbing until hands are dry (approximately 20 seconds)

**Key points:**
- Use products containing 60-95% alcohol
- More effective than soap and water for most situations
- Faster than handwashing
- Less irritating to skin with repeated use
- Accessible at point of care

#### B. Handwashing with Soap and Water
**When to use (required instead of ABHR):**
- Hands are visibly soiled or contaminated with blood/body fluids
- Before eating and after using the restroom
- After caring for patients with known or suspected C. difficile infection
- After caring for patients with known or suspected norovirus
- After contact with patients with suspected or confirmed infectious diarrhea

**Technique:**
1. Wet hands with clean running water (warm or cold)
2. Apply soap
3. Lather and scrub all surfaces of hands for at least 20 seconds
4. Rinse hands thoroughly under running water
5. Dry hands with a clean towel or air dry
6. Use towel to turn off faucet if available

### 3.5 Glove Use and Hand Hygiene

- Gloves are NOT a substitute for hand hygiene
- Perform hand hygiene before donning gloves
- Perform hand hygiene after removing gloves
- Change gloves between tasks on the same patient when moving from contaminated to clean body sites
- Change gloves and perform hand hygiene between patients
- Never wash or reuse disposable gloves

### 3.6 Skin Care Considerations

- Frequent hand hygiene can cause skin irritation and dermatitis
- Use moisturizing lotions to maintain skin integrity
- Healthcare facilities should provide skin-friendly products
- Report persistent skin problems to occupational health
- Damaged skin can harbor more microorganisms

### 3.7 Hand Hygiene Compliance and Monitoring

- Healthcare facilities should monitor and track hand hygiene compliance
- Provide regular feedback to healthcare personnel
- Use multimodal strategies to improve compliance
- Ensure easy access to hand hygiene supplies at point of care
- Address barriers to compliance (time, access, skin irritation)

### 3.8 Key Recommendations for Home Health Caregivers

1. Carry alcohol-based hand rub and use it before and after every patient contact
2. Ensure access to soap and water in the patient's home
3. Perform hand hygiene at minimum at the "5 Moments" described above
4. Maintain healthy skin on hands through regular moisturizing
5. Do not wear artificial nails or nail extensions when providing direct patient care
6. Keep natural nails short (less than 1/4 inch)
7. Remove rings and hand jewelry when performing hand hygiene (or at minimum, ensure hand hygiene product reaches skin under jewelry)
8. Educate patients and families about the importance of hand hygiene
