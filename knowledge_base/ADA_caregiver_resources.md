### Overview

The ADA provides a dedicated resource section for people who care for someone with diabetes. The content is aimed at family caregivers, professional caregivers, and care partners who help manage diabetes in a person who may not be fully independent in their self-care. This is particularly relevant for elderly adults with diabetes who may have cognitive impairment, physical limitations, or multiple chronic conditions.

### Key Themes

- Diabetes management is a daily, ongoing commitment that requires consistent attention to diet, activity, medication, and monitoring
- Caregivers are often the front line for recognizing warning signs of blood sugar emergencies
- ADA emphasizes that hypoglycemia (low blood sugar) is a medical emergency that caregivers must be prepared to recognize and respond to immediately
- The emotional burden of diabetes management on both the patient and caregiver is explicitly acknowledged
- Resources are written in accessible, non-medical language for lay caregivers

## 2. Diabetes Management Basics for Caregivers

- **URL:** https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes
- **Related URLs:** https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/type-2, https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/type-1
- **Page Title:** About Diabetes | American Diabetes Association

### 2.1 Understanding Diabetes Types

#### Type 2 Diabetes (Most Common in Older Adults)
- The body does not use insulin effectively (insulin resistance), and over time may not produce enough insulin
- Most common form of diabetes, accounting for approximately 90-95% of all diabetes cases
- More common in older adults, people with overweight/obesity, those with a family history, and certain racial/ethnic groups
- Can often be managed with lifestyle changes (diet, exercise) and oral medications, though some patients require insulin
- Progression varies; management needs typically increase over time

#### Type 1 Diabetes
- The body produces little or no insulin due to autoimmune destruction of insulin-producing cells
- Requires insulin for survival (injections or insulin pump)
- Less common overall but does occur in older adults
- Caregivers must be especially vigilant about insulin administration and blood sugar monitoring

#### Other Relevant Forms
- **Prediabetes:** Blood sugar is higher than normal but not yet in the diabetes range; lifestyle changes can prevent or delay progression to Type 2
- **Gestational diabetes:** Develops during pregnancy (less relevant for elderly care)
- **Steroid-induced diabetes:** Blood sugar elevations caused by corticosteroid medications; relevant for patients on prednisone or similar drugs

### 2.2 Blood Sugar Monitoring

Blood glucose monitoring is a cornerstone of diabetes management. Caregivers may need to assist with or perform monitoring:

#### How to Monitor
- **Fingerstick blood glucose testing:** Use a blood glucose meter; clean the finger with alcohol, prick with a lancet, apply blood to the test strip, record the result
- **Continuous Glucose Monitors (CGMs):** Wearable devices that track blood sugar in real time; some require fingerstick calibration; caregivers should learn to read and interpret CGM data and respond to alerts
- **Timing of checks:** As directed by the healthcare team; commonly before meals, 2 hours after meals, at bedtime, and any time symptoms of low or high blood sugar occur

#### Target Blood Sugar Ranges (General -- individualized targets set by the healthcare team)

| Timing | General Target Range |
|--------|---------------------|
| Fasting / before meals | 80-130 mg/dL |
| 2 hours after start of meal | Less than 180 mg/dL |
| Bedtime | 100-140 mg/dL |

**Important note:** Targets for older adults may be less strict (e.g., fasting 100-180 mg/dL) depending on health status, life expectancy, and risk of hypoglycemia. The ADA Standards of Care recommend individualized glycemic targets for older adults, with less stringent targets for those with complex health needs or limited life expectancy to reduce the risk of hypoglycemia.

#### What Affects Blood Sugar
- **Raises blood sugar:** Food (especially carbohydrates), stress, illness, infection, certain medications (steroids, some diuretics), decreased physical activity, inadequate diabetes medication
- **Lowers blood sugar:** Diabetes medications (especially insulin and sulfonylureas), physical activity, delayed or skipped meals, alcohol, weight loss

#### Recording and Sharing Results
- Keep a log of blood sugar readings with the date, time, and any relevant notes (meals, activity, illness, medication changes)
- Bring the log to all healthcare appointments
- Share concerning patterns with the healthcare team promptly

### 2.3 A1C (Hemoglobin A1C)

- A1C is a blood test that reflects average blood sugar over the past 2-3 months
- Typically checked every 3-6 months by the healthcare provider
- **General target:** Less than 7% for most adults; for older adults with multiple comorbidities, the target may be less strict (less than 8% or even less than 8.5%), as recommended by ADA Standards of Care for older adults
- Caregivers should understand the patient's A1C target and what the result means for ongoing management

## 3. Hypoglycemia Recognition and Response

- **URL:** https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/hypoglycemia
- **Page Title:** Hypoglycemia (Low Blood Sugar) | American Diabetes Association

### CRITICAL CAREGIVER KNOWLEDGE -- HYPOGLYCEMIA IS A MEDICAL EMERGENCY

Hypoglycemia (blood sugar below 70 mg/dL) is one of the most important topics for caregivers of people with diabetes. It can occur rapidly and requires immediate action.

### 3.1 Causes of Hypoglycemia

- Taking too much insulin or certain oral diabetes medications (sulfonylureas such as glipizide, glyburide, glimepiride)
- Delayed, missed, or insufficient meals or snacks
- Increased physical activity without adjusting food or medication
- Alcohol consumption (especially on an empty stomach)
- Hot weather
- Changes in routine (e.g., illness, stress)
- Impaired kidney function (affects medication clearance)

### 3.2 Signs and Symptoms of Hypoglycemia

Caregivers should learn to recognize these signs, as the person may not always be aware of their own low blood sugar (a condition called hypoglycemia unawareness, which is more common in older adults and those with longstanding diabetes):

#### Mild to Moderate Symptoms (Blood Sugar 54-70 mg/dL)
- Shakiness or trembling
- Sweating (may be cold and clammy)
- Rapid heartbeat (palpitations)
- Hunger (often sudden and intense)
- Irritability, mood changes, or anxiety
- Dizziness or lightheadedness
- Pale skin
- Tingling or numbness in lips, tongue, or cheeks
- Headache
- Difficulty concentrating or confusion

#### Severe Symptoms (Blood Sugar Below 54 mg/dL)
- Confusion or disorientation
- Slurred speech
- Blurred or double vision
- Clumsiness or difficulty with coordination
- Inability to eat or drink safely
- Seizures
- Loss of consciousness
- **This is a medical emergency requiring immediate action**

### 3.3 Treating Hypoglycemia -- The Rule of 15

ADA recommends the "15-15 Rule" for treating mild to moderate hypoglycemia:

#### Step-by-Step Response

1. **CHECK:** If possible, check blood sugar to confirm it is below 70 mg/dL. If you cannot check, treat based on symptoms.
2. **TREAT with 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate:**
   - 4 glucose tablets (preferred)
   - 4 oz (1/2 cup) of fruit juice (not diet/sugar-free)
   - 4 oz (1/2 cup) of regular soda (not diet)
   - 1 tablespoon of sugar or honey
   - Hard candies (check labels for amount equaling 15g carbohydrate)
3. **WAIT 15 minutes**, then recheck blood sugar
4. **REPEAT** if blood sugar is still below 70 mg/dL: Give another 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate
5. **FOLLOW UP:** Once blood sugar is above 70 mg/dL, eat a snack or meal within the next hour to prevent recurrence (e.g., crackers with peanut butter, cheese and crackers, half a sandwich)

#### What NOT To Do
- Do NOT use chocolate, cookies, or other high-fat foods as the first-line treatment (fat slows sugar absorption)
- Do NOT give food or liquid to an unconscious or semi-conscious person (choking risk)
- Do NOT delay treatment while looking for a blood glucose meter

### 3.4 Severe Hypoglycemia -- Emergency Response

If the person is confused, unable to swallow safely, having seizures, or unconscious:

1. **Do NOT put anything in the person's mouth** (risk of choking)
2. **Administer glucagon** if available and you have been trained:
   - **Glucagon injection kit:** Follow the instructions in the kit; inject into the thigh, upper arm, or buttocks
   - **Nasal glucagon (Baqsimi):** Administer one spray into one nostril (no need to inhale; it absorbs through the nasal lining)
   - **Auto-injector glucagon (Gvoke):** Follow device instructions
3. **Call 911** if:
   - No glucagon is available
   - You have not been trained to use glucagon
   - The person does not respond to glucagon within 15 minutes
   - The person is not breathing or is having a prolonged seizure
4. **Place the person on their side** (recovery position) to prevent choking if they vomit
5. **Stay with the person** until emergency help arrives or they recover
6. **Once conscious and able to swallow:** Give a fast-acting carbohydrate followed by a snack or meal

### 3.5 Hypoglycemia Preparedness for Caregivers

- **Always keep fast-acting glucose readily available** -- in the home, in the car, in a bag when going out; glucose tablets are the most reliable and portable option
- **Know where glucagon is stored** and ensure it is not expired; practice using it before an emergency
- **Ensure the person wears medical identification** (bracelet or necklace) indicating they have diabetes
- **Inform family members, coworkers, and frequent contacts** about how to recognize and respond to low blood sugar
- **Report all hypoglycemic episodes** to the healthcare team, especially recurrent episodes or severe events
- **Understand hypoglycemia unawareness:** Some patients lose the ability to feel early warning symptoms; these patients need more frequent blood sugar monitoring and may need adjusted medication or glycemic targets

## 4. Hyperglycemia Recognition and Response

- **URL:** https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/hyperglycemia
- **Page Title:** Hyperglycemia (High Blood Sugar) | American Diabetes Association

### 4.1 Understanding Hyperglycemia

Hyperglycemia (blood sugar above target range) can develop slowly and may not cause obvious symptoms until blood sugar is significantly elevated. Chronic hyperglycemia contributes to long-term diabetes complications.

### 4.2 Causes of Hyperglycemia

- Eating more than usual or eating high-carbohydrate foods
- Less physical activity than usual
- Missed or insufficient diabetes medication
- Illness, infection, or stress (the body releases hormones that raise blood sugar)
- Certain medications (corticosteroids, some diuretics)
- Dawn phenomenon (natural rise in blood sugar in the early morning hours)
- Insulin that has gone bad (expired, exposed to extreme temperatures)

### 4.3 Signs and Symptoms

#### Early Symptoms
- Increased thirst (polydipsia)
- Frequent urination (polyuria)
- Blurred vision
- Fatigue
- Headache

#### Later / More Severe Symptoms
- Nausea and vomiting
- Fruity-scented breath (ketoacidosis; more common in Type 1)
- Shortness of breath
- Dry mouth
- Abdominal pain
- Confusion or difficulty concentrating
- Unexplained weight loss

### 4.4 Caregiver Response to Hyperglycemia

#### Mild to Moderate High Blood Sugar (180-300 mg/dL)
- Encourage the person to drink water (unless fluid-restricted)
- Check whether medications were taken as prescribed
- Review recent food intake for excessive carbohydrates
- Encourage light physical activity if the person feels well enough and blood sugar is below 250 mg/dL
- Recheck blood sugar in 1-2 hours
- Contact the healthcare provider if blood sugar remains elevated despite appropriate action

#### Persistent or Severe High Blood Sugar (Above 300 mg/dL)
- Check for ketones in urine if the person has Type 1 diabetes or if advised by the healthcare team (use ketone test strips)
- Encourage water intake
- Do NOT encourage exercise if blood sugar is above 250 mg/dL with ketones present
- Contact the healthcare provider for guidance on medication adjustment

### 4.5 Diabetic Emergencies -- When to Call 911

#### Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) -- More Common in Type 1
- Blood sugar very high (often above 300 mg/dL)
- Moderate to large ketones in urine or blood
- Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
- Fruity-scented breath
- Deep, rapid breathing (Kussmaul breathing)
- Confusion or loss of consciousness
- **This is a life-threatening emergency -- call 911 immediately**

#### Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS) -- More Common in Type 2 / Older Adults
- Blood sugar very high (often above 600 mg/dL)
- Extreme dehydration
- Confusion, disorientation, or altered consciousness
- Vision changes
- Hallucinations
- Seizures
- Can develop over days to weeks, especially during illness
- **This is a life-threatening emergency -- call 911 immediately**
- **Note for caregivers:** HHS is particularly relevant for elderly patients with Type 2 diabetes, especially those who may not drink enough fluids during illness or who are unable to communicate their symptoms

## 5. Nutrition and Meal Planning for Caregivers

- **URL:** https://diabetes.org/food-nutrition
- **Related URL:** https://diabetes.org/food-nutrition/meal-planning
- **Page Title:** Food & Nutrition | American Diabetes Association

### 5.1 Key Nutritional Principles

#### Carbohydrate Awareness
- Carbohydrates have the greatest impact on blood sugar
- Caregivers should understand which foods contain carbohydrates: grains, bread, pasta, rice, cereals, fruits, starchy vegetables (potatoes, corn, peas), beans, milk, yogurt, sweets, and sugary drinks
- **Portion control** of carbohydrates is essential; the healthcare team or a registered dietitian can help set appropriate carbohydrate goals per meal
- The **Diabetes Plate Method** (ADA) is a simple tool: fill half the plate with non-starchy vegetables, one quarter with lean protein, and one quarter with carbohydrate foods; add water or a low-calorie drink

#### Reading Food Labels
- Check serving size, total carbohydrates, added sugars, sodium, and fiber
- Compare similar products and choose those with lower added sugars and sodium
- Be cautious with "sugar-free" products, which may still contain carbohydrates

#### Meal Timing and Consistency
- Consistent meal timing helps manage blood sugar, especially for patients on insulin or sulfonylureas
- Avoid skipping meals, which can lead to hypoglycemia or overeating later
- Plan snacks if there will be a long gap between meals

### 5.2 Practical Meal Planning Tips for Caregivers

- Plan meals for the week in advance; this reduces stress and improves nutritional quality
- Cook with less added salt, sugar, and saturated fat
- Keep healthy snacks available for between meals (nuts, cheese and whole-grain crackers, vegetables with hummus, a piece of fruit with a small handful of nuts)
- When preparing meals for the whole family, the same diabetes-friendly meals are generally healthy for everyone
- Use cooking methods that reduce fat: baking, grilling, steaming, broiling rather than frying
- Limit sweetened beverages (juice, soda, sweetened tea); offer water, unsweetened tea, or sparkling water
- If the person has a poor appetite (common in older adults), focus on nutrient-dense foods and discuss strategies with the healthcare team

### 5.3 Special Dietary Considerations

- **Co-existing heart failure:** Sodium restriction in addition to carbohydrate management
- **Kidney disease (diabetic nephropathy):** May require protein, potassium, and phosphorus restrictions; always follow the dietitian's guidance
- **Texture modifications:** For patients with swallowing difficulties (dysphagia), adapt diabetes-friendly foods to the prescribed textures; consult the speech therapist and dietitian
- **Cultural food preferences:** Adapt diabetes management principles to the person's preferred cuisine and cultural food practices

## 6. Foot Care and Skin Care

- **URL:** https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/complications/foot-complications
- **Page Title:** Foot Complications | American Diabetes Association

### 6.1 Why Foot Care Matters in Diabetes

- Diabetes can cause peripheral neuropathy (nerve damage) leading to reduced or absent sensation in the feet; the person may not feel cuts, blisters, or sores
- Diabetes can impair blood circulation to the feet (peripheral arterial disease), slowing wound healing
- Even minor foot injuries can lead to serious infections and, in severe cases, may require amputation
- Prevention through daily inspection and proper foot care is essential

### 6.2 Daily Foot Inspection -- Caregiver Checklist

Caregivers should perform or assist with a daily foot check, examining both feet for:

- **Cuts, scrapes, or blisters**
- **Redness, warmth, or swelling** (may indicate infection or pressure)
- **Calluses or corns** (should not be self-treated; see a podiatrist)
- **Dry or cracked skin** (especially on the heels)
- **Ingrown toenails or nail changes**
- **Color changes** (pale, blue, red, or darkened areas)
- **Temperature differences** between feet (one foot warmer than the other may indicate infection or circulation problems)
- **Sores or wounds that are not healing**
- **Drainage, odor, or swelling**
- **Changes in foot shape** (may indicate Charcot foot, a serious complication)

**Tip:** Use a mirror to check the bottoms of the feet, or have the caregiver perform this check directly.

### 6.3 Foot Care Best Practices

- **Wash feet daily** in lukewarm water (test temperature with the elbow, not the feet, due to neuropathy); do not soak feet (softens skin excessively)
- **Dry feet thoroughly**, especially between the toes
- **Apply moisturizer** to tops and bottoms of feet to prevent cracking, but NOT between the toes (moisture between toes promotes fungal infections)
- **Trim toenails** straight across, file edges gently; if nails are thick or difficult to trim, have a podiatrist do this
- **Never go barefoot** -- wear shoes or slippers at all times, even indoors
- **Check shoes before putting them on** for pebbles, rough areas, or other objects that could cause injury
- **Wear well-fitting shoes** with room for the toes; avoid tight shoes, pointed toes, and high heels; break in new shoes gradually
- **Wear clean, dry socks** without tight elastic bands; moisture-wicking materials are preferred; change socks daily
- **Avoid heating pads, hot water bottles, and electric blankets** on the feet (risk of burns due to neuropathy)
- **Do not use over-the-counter corn or callus removers** (chemical agents can damage skin)
- **Do not attempt bathroom surgery** -- never cut calluses or try to remove ingrown toenails at home

### 6.4 When to Escalate Foot Concerns

#### Call the Healthcare Provider Same Day
- Any new cut, sore, or blister that is not healing
- Redness, warmth, or swelling that is increasing
- Any drainage (especially if discolored or foul-smelling)
- New pain or discomfort in the feet (even if the person usually has reduced sensation, pain can indicate serious problems)
- Color changes (darkening, white spots, blue/purple areas)
- Temperature difference between the two feet
- Signs of fungal infection (athlete's foot): itching, cracking, peeling between toes

#### Call 911 or Go to Emergency
- Sudden loss of feeling or movement in the foot
- Foot or leg that is cold, blue, or pale (possible critical limb ischemia)
- Signs of severe infection: red streaks extending from a wound, high fever, chills, confusion

### 6.5 General Skin Care in Diabetes

- Diabetes increases susceptibility to skin infections (bacterial and fungal)
- Keep skin clean and dry; moisturize regularly to prevent cracking
- Treat minor cuts immediately: clean with soap and water, apply antibiotic ointment, cover with a bandage; monitor for signs of infection
- Check skin folds (under breasts, abdomen, groin) for redness or fungal infections
- Report any slow-healing wounds to the healthcare provider

## 7. Exercise and Physical Activity

- **URL:** https://diabetes.org/health-wellness/fitness
- **Page Title:** Fitness | American Diabetes Association

### 7.1 Benefits of Physical Activity in Diabetes

- Helps lower blood sugar by increasing insulin sensitivity
- Improves cardiovascular health (critical since people with diabetes have higher cardiovascular risk)
- Helps manage weight
- Improves mood and reduces stress
- Helps with blood pressure and cholesterol management
- Improves strength, balance, and flexibility (important for fall prevention in older adults)

### 7.2 ADA General Recommendations

- At least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity (brisk walking, swimming, cycling), spread over at least 3 days with no more than 2 consecutive days without activity
- Resistance (strength) training at least 2 days per week
- Reduce prolonged sitting; break up sitting time with light activity every 30 minutes
- For older adults: include balance and flexibility exercises to reduce fall risk
- Always consult the healthcare provider before starting a new exercise program

### 7.3 Safety Considerations for Caregivers

#### Blood Sugar and Exercise

- **Check blood sugar before exercise** if the person takes insulin or sulfonylureas
  - If below 100 mg/dL: eat a small snack (15-20g carbohydrate) before exercising
  - If above 250 mg/dL with ketones present: do NOT exercise (risk of DKA)
  - If above 300 mg/dL (even without ketones): use caution; consult the healthcare team
- **Check blood sugar during and after exercise**, especially when starting a new activity or increasing intensity
- **Carry fast-acting glucose** during exercise in case of hypoglycemia
- Hypoglycemia can occur during exercise or up to several hours after exercise (delayed hypoglycemia)

#### Other Exercise Safety
- Ensure the person wears proper footwear (especially important due to neuropathy)
- Stay hydrated
- Avoid exercising in extreme heat or cold
- Watch for signs of overexertion: excessive shortness of breath, chest pain, dizziness, or unusual fatigue
- If the person has heart disease, peripheral neuropathy, retinopathy (eye disease), or nephropathy (kidney disease), the exercise program should be adapted; follow healthcare provider guidance

### 7.4 Practical Activity Ideas for Older Adults with Diabetes

- Walking (outdoors or in a mall for climate-controlled environment)
- Chair exercises (seated stretching, arm exercises with light weights)
- Water aerobics or pool walking
- Tai Chi (improves balance, reduces fall risk)
- Light gardening or household tasks
- Gentle yoga or stretching
- Dancing
- Resistance bands for strength training

## 8. Medication Awareness for Caregivers

- **URL:** https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/medication-management
- **Related URL:** https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/insulin
- **Page Title:** Medication Management | American Diabetes Association

### 8.1 Important Note on Scope

CareExpert AI does NOT provide medication dosing advice. The information below is for caregiver awareness and education. All medication decisions should be made by the healthcare provider.

### 8.2 Common Types of Diabetes Medications

#### Oral Medications (Pills)
- **Metformin:** Most commonly prescribed first-line medication for Type 2 diabetes; helps reduce blood sugar by decreasing glucose production in the liver and improving insulin sensitivity; generally does not cause hypoglycemia when used alone; common side effects include GI upset (nausea, diarrhea)
- **Sulfonylureas (glipizide, glyburide, glimepiride):** Stimulate the pancreas to produce more insulin; CAN cause hypoglycemia -- caregivers must be vigilant about meal timing and blood sugar monitoring
- **SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin):** Help kidneys remove excess sugar through urine; may cause urinary tract infections and genital yeast infections; also have cardiovascular and kidney-protective benefits; may cause dehydration in older adults
- **DPP-4 inhibitors (sitagliptin, saxagliptin, linagliptin):** Help the body produce more insulin when blood sugar is high; generally well-tolerated; low risk of hypoglycemia
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists (injectable: semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide):** Help lower blood sugar, may cause weight loss, and have cardiovascular benefits; common side effects include nausea, especially when starting; available as weekly or daily injections
- **Thiazolidinediones (pioglitazone):** Improve insulin sensitivity; can cause weight gain, fluid retention (use with caution in heart failure)

#### Insulin
- Required for all Type 1 diabetes and some Type 2 diabetes patients
- Types: rapid-acting (lispro, aspart), short-acting (regular), intermediate-acting (NPH), long-acting (glargine, detemir, degludec), and premixed combinations
- **Storage:** Unopened insulin should be refrigerated (not frozen); opened insulin can typically be stored at room temperature for up to 28 days (varies by type -- check the label); never use insulin that appears cloudy when it should be clear, or has particles or discoloration
- **Injection technique:** Rotate injection sites to prevent lipodystrophy (lumps under the skin); do not inject into scar tissue, bruised areas, or areas with lumps
- **CAN cause hypoglycemia** -- caregivers managing insulin must be trained in hypoglycemia recognition and response

### 8.3 Medication Management Tips for Caregivers

- Maintain a current list of all medications (diabetes and non-diabetes), including dose, timing, and prescribing provider
- Use a pill organizer and/or set alarms/reminders for medication times
- Ensure medications are refilled before they run out
- Store medications properly (refrigerate insulin; keep oral medications in a cool, dry place away from humidity)
- Monitor for side effects and report them to the healthcare provider
- **Never adjust medication doses** without healthcare provider guidance
- Be aware of drug interactions (some OTC medications, like certain cold medicines, can affect blood sugar)
- Bring the medication list to every healthcare appointment

## 9. Diabetes Complications Awareness

- **URL:** https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/complications
- **Page Title:** Complications | American Diabetes Association

### 9.1 Overview

Chronically elevated blood sugar can damage blood vessels and nerves throughout the body. Caregivers should be aware of common complications to monitor for early signs and support prevention.

### 9.2 Key Complications Caregivers Should Know About

#### Peripheral Neuropathy (Nerve Damage)
- Numbness, tingling, pain, or loss of sensation in the feet and hands
- Increases fall risk (the person may not feel the ground properly)
- Increases risk of undetected foot injuries
- **Caregiver action:** Daily foot inspections, proper footwear, fall prevention measures

#### Cardiovascular Disease
- People with diabetes have 2-4 times higher risk of heart disease and stroke
- **Caregiver action:** Support blood pressure and cholesterol management, promote physical activity, recognize heart attack and stroke warning signs (see AHA resources), support medication adherence

#### Retinopathy (Eye Disease)
- Damage to blood vessels in the retina; can lead to vision loss
- **Caregiver action:** Ensure annual dilated eye exams; report any sudden vision changes immediately

#### Nephropathy (Kidney Disease)
- Damage to the kidneys' filtering system; can progress to kidney failure
- Early stages often have no symptoms
- **Caregiver action:** Ensure regular lab work (kidney function tests, urine albumin); support blood pressure management; follow dietary guidelines from the healthcare team

#### Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD)
- Reduced blood flow to the legs and feet
- Symptoms: leg pain with walking (claudication), cold feet, slow-healing sores
- **Caregiver action:** Daily foot checks, report symptoms of PAD, ensure appropriate footwear

#### Dental and Gum Disease
- Diabetes increases risk of gum disease (periodontitis), which can also worsen blood sugar control
- **Caregiver action:** Support daily oral hygiene (brushing twice daily, flossing); ensure regular dental visits

#### Infections
- Diabetes impairs immune function, increasing susceptibility to infections (UTIs, skin infections, respiratory infections)
- **Caregiver action:** Practice good hygiene (hand washing, skin care), recognize signs of infection (fever, redness, swelling, unusual discharge, increased confusion in older adults), report promptly

### 9.3 Prevention of Complications

The ADA emphasizes the "ABCs" of diabetes care:

- **A -- A1C:** Keep hemoglobin A1C at or below the individualized target
- **B -- Blood pressure:** Target is generally below 130/80 mmHg (individualized)
- **C -- Cholesterol:** Manage LDL cholesterol, often with statin therapy for adults with diabetes over age 40
- **s -- Smoking cessation:** If the person smokes, support quitting; connect with cessation resources

Additional prevention measures:
- Regular healthcare visits (at least twice yearly for diabetes management)
- Annual eye exam (dilated)
- Annual comprehensive foot exam (by healthcare provider)
- Regular dental care
- Up-to-date immunizations (flu, pneumonia, COVID-19, shingles)
- Kidney function monitoring (annual urine albumin and eGFR)

## 10. Caregiver Self-Care and Support

- **URL:** https://diabetes.org/tools-resources/for-caregivers
- **Related resources throughout diabetes.org**

### 10.1 The Emotional Burden of Diabetes Caregiving

- Managing someone else's diabetes is demanding and involves constant vigilance (meal planning, medication timing, blood sugar monitoring, foot care)
- Caregivers may feel anxious about the possibility of hypoglycemic emergencies, especially at night
- Frustration can arise when the care recipient does not follow dietary recommendations or resists management
- The invisible nature of diabetes caregiving (it does not always look like "caregiving") can lead to under-recognition of the caregiver's burden
- Caregivers of people with diabetes and comorbid conditions (dementia, heart failure, mobility limitations) face compounded demands

### 10.2 Self-Care Recommendations

- **Educate yourself:** Understanding diabetes and its management reduces anxiety and increases confidence
- **Attend diabetes education programs:** ADA-recognized education programs teach practical management skills and are often available to caregivers as well as patients
- **Build a care team:** Work with the person's healthcare provider, diabetes educator, dietitian, and pharmacist; you do not have to manage everything alone
- **Set realistic expectations:** Perfect blood sugar control is not achievable; aim for "good enough" management within the target range most of the time
- **Join a support group:** Connect with other diabetes caregivers (ADA community, local support groups, online forums)
- **Maintain your own health:** Keep your own medical appointments, exercise, eat well, and get adequate sleep
- **Ask for help:** Enlist other family members or friends for specific tasks; consider professional home health aide support
- **Take breaks:** Arrange for someone else to handle diabetes management responsibilities periodically so you can rest and recharge
