## 1. PHI HHA Training Curriculum

### 1.1 Program Overview

- **Full Name:** PHI Home Health Aide Training Curriculum (formerly HAWI -- Home Aide Workforce Initiative)
- **Organization:** PHI (Paraprofessional Healthcare Institute), formerly known as the Paraprofessional Healthcare Institute
- **Headquarters:** Bronx, New York
- **Website:** https://www.phinational.org/
- **Curriculum URL:** https://www.phinational.org/wp-content/uploads/legacy/hawi/hawi-introduction.pdf (introduction module; full curriculum requires purchase)
- **Target Audience:** Home health aides (HHAs) and personal care aides (PCAs) in formal employment settings
- **Training Hours:** Designed to meet or exceed the federal 75-hour minimum (42 CFR 484.80), with many implementations extending beyond this
- **Regulatory Alignment:** Meets CMS Conditions of Participation requirements and NYS DOH HHA training requirements

### 1.2 Organizational Significance

PHI is the leading national organization focused on the direct care workforce (home health aides, personal care aides, nursing assistants). Founded in 1991, PHI conducts research, advocates for workforce policy, and develops training programs. Their curriculum is widely regarded as the gold standard for HHA training in the United States and has been adopted by home care agencies, community colleges, and workforce development programs across multiple states. PHI's approach is distinctive for integrating person-centered care philosophy, problem-solving skills, and worker empowerment into technical caregiving instruction.

### 1.3 Curriculum Structure and Pedagogical Approach

PHI's curriculum uses a **competency-based, learner-centered** approach. Key pedagogical features include:

- **Active learning methods:** Role-play, case studies, small-group discussions, hands-on practice
- **Coaching model:** Instructors function as coaches rather than lecturers, facilitating peer learning
- **Problem-solving emphasis:** Trainees learn to analyze situations and develop solutions rather than memorize scripted responses
- **Person-centered philosophy:** Every module integrates dignity, autonomy, and respect for care recipients
- **Cultural competence:** Content addresses diverse populations, cultural sensitivity, and communication across differences
- **Reflective practice:** Trainees reflect on their experiences and emotional responses to caregiving situations

### 1.4 Curriculum Modules and Topics

The PHI curriculum is organized into the following major modules and content areas:

#### Module 1: Role of the Home Health Aide
- Definition and scope of the HHA role
- The home care team: HHAs, RNs, therapists, social workers, physicians
- Supervision structure and reporting relationships
- Legal and ethical responsibilities
- Boundaries and professionalism
- Scope of practice: what HHAs can and cannot do
- Confidentiality and privacy (HIPAA basics)
- Client rights (dignity, autonomy, informed consent, refusal of care)
- Abuse and neglect recognition and mandatory reporting
- Documentation and record-keeping

#### Module 2: Communication and Interpersonal Skills
- Active listening techniques
- Verbal and non-verbal communication
- Communicating with clients who have hearing, vision, or cognitive impairments
- Communicating with families and supervisors
- SBAR communication framework (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation)
- Cultural sensitivity and working with diverse populations
- Managing conflict and difficult conversations
- Empathy and emotional intelligence in caregiving
- Language barriers and use of interpreters

#### Module 3: Infection Control
- Chain of infection (agent, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry, susceptible host)
- Standard precautions and transmission-based precautions
- Hand hygiene: technique, timing (5 moments), soap vs. hand sanitizer
- Personal protective equipment (PPE): gloves, masks, gowns
- Proper donning and doffing procedures
- Bloodborne pathogen awareness
- Safe handling and disposal of sharps and contaminated materials
- Common infections in the elderly (UTIs, pneumonia, skin infections)
- COVID-19 and respiratory illness awareness
- Cleaning and disinfection in the home setting
- Food safety and safe food handling

#### Module 4: Safety and Emergency Procedures
- Home safety assessment (room-by-room approach)
- Fall prevention strategies and environmental modifications
- Fire safety, evacuation planning, smoke detector maintenance
- Poisoning prevention
- Electrical safety
- Emergency procedures: when and how to call 911
- Basic first aid awareness (choking, bleeding, burns)
- Recognizing emergencies: stroke (FAST), heart attack, diabetic emergencies, seizures
- Disaster preparedness in the home setting
- Safe use of oxygen equipment in the home
- Body mechanics for caregivers: lifting, transferring, injury prevention

#### Module 5: Body Structure and Function
- Overview of body systems (musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, respiratory, nervous, digestive, urinary, endocrine, integumentary)
- Normal aging changes by body system
- Common conditions in older adults (arthritis, hypertension, diabetes, COPD, heart failure, stroke, dementia)
- Signs and symptoms to observe and report for each system
- Understanding vital signs: temperature, pulse, respiration, blood pressure
- Taking and recording vital signs

#### Module 6: Personal Care and Activities of Daily Living
- **Bathing:** bed bath, tub bath, shower bath; safety precautions; preserving dignity and modesty
- **Skin care:** inspection during bathing, moisturizing, recognizing skin breakdown and pressure injuries
- **Oral hygiene:** brushing, denture care, mouth care for unconscious patients
- **Hair care:** washing, grooming, cultural considerations
- **Shaving:** safety considerations, electric vs. manual razor
- **Nail care:** fingernail and toenail care, precautions for diabetic clients
- **Dressing and undressing:** assisting with clothing, adaptive techniques, one-sided dressing for stroke patients
- **Toileting:** assisting with bedpan, urinal, commode, toilet transfer; incontinence care
- **Incontinence management:** types of incontinence, products, skin protection, dignity
- **Catheter care awareness:** external catheter care, recognizing problems, when to escalate (HHAs do not insert catheters)

#### Module 7: Nutrition and Meal Preparation
- Basic nutritional needs of older adults
- Food groups and healthy meal planning
- Dietary modifications for common conditions (diabetic diet, low-sodium, renal, texture-modified)
- Thickened liquids and swallowing precautions
- Proper positioning during eating
- Choking prevention and recognition
- Adaptive utensils and eating aids
- Monitoring food and fluid intake
- Signs of dehydration and malnutrition
- Food safety: storage, preparation, temperature
- Cultural and religious dietary considerations

#### Module 8: Mobility, Transfers, and Positioning
- Body mechanics for caregivers (proper lifting, bending, reaching)
- Bed mobility: turning, repositioning, moving up in bed
- Transfer techniques: bed to chair, chair to toilet, chair to car
- Stand-pivot transfers
- Use of gait belts
- Assistive devices: walkers, canes, wheelchairs (proper use and fitting)
- Ambulation assistance
- Range of motion exercises (passive and active-assisted)
- Positioning for comfort and pressure injury prevention (Fowler's, lateral, supine, prone)
- Repositioning schedules
- Mechanical lift awareness (Hoyer lift operation, when applicable)
- Wheelchair safety and management

#### Module 9: Mental Health and Cognitive Impairment
- Understanding dementia and Alzheimer's disease
- Stages of dementia: early, middle, late
- Behavioral symptoms: agitation, wandering, sundowning, repetitive behaviors
- Communication techniques for people with dementia
- Activities and engagement for people with cognitive impairment
- Depression in older adults: signs, approach, when to report
- Anxiety and fear in care recipients
- Validation therapy basics
- Redirection techniques
- Managing resistance to care
- Supporting dignity and personhood in dementia care

#### Module 10: Special Populations and Conditions
- Diabetes care awareness (blood sugar signs, foot care, dietary needs)
- Heart failure management basics (weight monitoring, sodium restriction, activity)
- Stroke recovery support (ADL adaptations, communication with aphasia, swallowing safety)
- COPD and respiratory conditions (oxygen safety, breathing techniques, activity pacing)
- Arthritis and chronic pain (comfort measures, activity modification)
- End-of-life care and comfort measures
- Advance directives awareness (DNR, healthcare proxy, living will)

#### Module 11: Observation, Reporting, and Documentation
- What to observe: changes in physical condition, behavior, mood, appetite, mobility
- How to report: to whom, when, what information to include
- SBAR framework for reporting
- Documentation requirements: what to record, when, accuracy and objectivity
- Reading and recording on flow sheets and care plans
- Understanding the plan of care
- Change-of-condition reporting triggers

#### Module 12: Caregiver Self-Care and Professional Development
- Recognizing signs of burnout and compassion fatigue
- Stress management techniques
- Setting professional boundaries
- Back injury prevention and physical self-care
- Emotional self-care and coping strategies
- Career pathways and advancement opportunities
- Continuing education and professional growth
- Workplace rights and advocacy

## 2. NYS DOH Home Care Aide Curriculum

### 2.1 Program Overview

- **Full Name:** New York State Department of Health Home Health Aide Training Program Curriculum
- **Organization:** New York State Department of Health (NYS DOH), Bureau of Home Care and Hospice Surveillance
- **Website:** https://www.health.ny.gov/professionals/home_care/
- **Curriculum URL:** https://www.health.ny.gov/professionals/home_care/curriculum/
- **Target Audience:** Home health aides seeking certification in New York State
- **Regulatory Authority:** NYS Public Health Law Article 36; 10 NYCRR Part 766 (Home Care Services)
- **Training Hours:** New York State requires a minimum of **75 hours** of training for HHAs, consistent with the federal CMS minimum under 42 CFR 484.80, with at least 16 hours of supervised practical (clinical) training

### 2.2 Regulatory Context

New York State's HHA training requirements are governed by:
- **Federal:** 42 CFR 484.80 (CMS Conditions of Participation for Home Health Agencies)
- **State:** 10 NYCRR Part 766 (Licensed Home Care Services Agencies) and Part 700 (Home Health Agency administration)
- NYS DOH approves training programs and maintains a Home Health Aide Registry
- Training programs must be approved by NYS DOH and conducted under RN supervision
- NYS has among the largest home care workforces in the nation, making its curriculum particularly influential

### 2.3 Curriculum Structure

The NYS DOH curriculum is organized into units that correspond to the federal training content requirements. The curriculum is structured as a state-approved standardized training program that home care agencies and training organizations must follow.

#### Unit 1: Introduction to Home Care
- The home care industry in New York State
- Types of home care agencies (CHHAs, LHCSAs, fiscal intermediaries)
- The home care team and interdisciplinary collaboration

- Relationship between aide, patient, family, and supervising nurse
- Legal responsibilities and ethical standards
- Patient/client rights under NYS regulations
- Confidentiality requirements

#### Unit 2: Fundamentals of Patient Care
- Basic human needs (physical, emotional, social, spiritual)
- Communication with patients, families, and healthcare team
- Cultural sensitivity and diversity awareness
- Observing and reporting changes in patient condition
- Documentation standards and requirements
- Understanding the care plan and following written instructions
- Time management in home care

#### Unit 3: Safety and Emergency Preparedness
- Home safety assessment and environmental hazard identification
- Fall prevention in the home environment
- Fire safety and evacuation procedures
- Electrical safety, poison prevention
- Safe use of medical equipment in the home (oxygen, hospital beds, wheelchairs)

#### Unit 4: Infection Prevention and Control
- Principles of infection control (chain of infection)

- Cleaning and disinfection of the home environment

#### Unit 5: Basic Body Structure and Function

- Common diseases and conditions in older adults
- Signs and symptoms to observe and report for each body system
- Understanding medical terminology basics

#### Unit 6: Vital Signs
- Measuring and recording temperature (oral, axillary, tympanic, temporal)

- Measuring and recording respiration (rate, depth, pattern)
- Understanding blood pressure (measurement may vary by agency/program scope)
- Normal ranges and when to report abnormal values
- Proper documentation of vital signs

#### Unit 7: Personal Care Skills
- **Bathing:** Complete bed bath, partial bath, tub bath, shower bath
- **Skin care:** Daily skin inspection, moisturizing, recognizing skin problems (redness, breakdown, rash)
- **Pressure injury prevention:** Repositioning schedules, pressure-relieving devices, skin inspection
- **Oral hygiene:** Tooth brushing, denture care, mouth care for dependent patients
- **Hair care:** Shampooing (in bed, at sink), brushing, grooming
- **Shaving:** Electric and safety razor techniques
- **Nail care:** Fingernail care (toenail care restrictions for diabetic patients)
- **Dressing and undressing:** Techniques for patients with limited mobility or one-sided weakness
- **Toileting:** Bedpan, urinal, bedside commode assistance
- **Incontinence care:** Perineal care, incontinence products, skin protection
- **Catheter care:** External catheter maintenance, intake/output awareness

#### Unit 8: Nutrition, Hydration, and Meal Preparation
- Nutritional needs of older adults
- MyPlate and dietary guidelines

- Feeding assistance techniques
- Proper positioning for meals
- Choking prevention and Heimlich maneuver awareness
- Monitoring intake (food and fluid)
- Signs of dehydration and malnutrition
- Food safety and safe food handling
- Grocery shopping and food storage

#### Unit 9: Mobility, Body Mechanics, and Transfers

- Transfer techniques (bed to chair, chair to toilet, stand-pivot)
- Use of gait belt

- Assistive devices: cane, walker, wheelchair, crutches
- Range of motion exercises (passive, active-assistive, active)
- Positioning in bed and chair (pressure relief positions)
- Wheelchair safety and transport
- Fall prevention during transfers and ambulation

#### Unit 10: Special Needs of the Cognitively Impaired Client

- Managing behavioral symptoms: agitation, wandering, sundowning, repetitive behaviors

#### Unit 11: Mental Health Considerations
- Depression in older adults
- Anxiety and adjustment disorders
- Substance abuse in the elderly
- Suicide risk awareness

#### Unit 12: Death and Dying / End-of-Life Care
- Understanding the dying process

#### Unit 13: Maternal and Child Health (if applicable)
- Basic newborn and infant care
- Maternal postpartum care
- Child safety and development basics
- Pediatric home care considerations
- Note: This unit may be included or excluded depending on the specific training program focus

#### Unit 14: Supervised Clinical Practice (minimum 16 hours)
- Hands-on practice of all personal care skills under RN observation
- Performed in a laboratory or clinical setting
- Skills checklist completion
- Competency demonstration and evaluation

### 2.4 Competency Requirements

NYS DOH requires competency evaluation in the following areas:

**Written/Oral Examination:**
- Communication skills
- Observation and reporting
- Infection control principles
- Safety and emergency procedures
- Basic body functioning
- Client rights and legal responsibilities

**Skills Demonstration (directly observed by RN):**
- Temperature, pulse, and respiration measurement and recording
- At least 3 personal care tasks (e.g., bed bath, oral care, transfer)
- Safe transfer and ambulation techniques
- Proper positioning
- Range of motion exercises
- Hand hygiene technique

**Ongoing Requirements:**
- Minimum 12 hours of in-service training per 12-month period
- Supervisory visits every 14 days by RN
- Competency re-evaluation as needed based on supervisory assessment
- Compliance with NYS Home Health Aide Registry requirements

## 3. REACH II Program (Resources for Enhancing Alzheimer's Caregiver Health)

### 3.1 Program Overview

- **Full Name:** Resources for Enhancing Alzheimer's Caregiver Health II (REACH II)
- **Organization:** National Institute on Aging (NIA), National Institutes of Health (NIH)
- **Principal Investigators:** Sharon H. Belle, PhD; Louis Burgio, PhD; and multiple site PIs including Richard Schulz, Laura N. Gitlin, William E. Haley, Alan Stevens, and David W. Coon
- **Study Sites:** 5 sites: Birmingham AL, Memphis TN, Miami FL, Palo Alto CA, Philadelphia PA
- **Landmark Publication:** Belle SH, Burgio L, Burns R, et al. Enhancing the quality of life of dementia caregivers from different ethnic or racial groups: a randomized, controlled trial. Ann Intern Med. 2006;145(10):727-738. (PMID: 17116917)
- **Also referenced as PMID:** 22417045 in existing knowledge base (may be secondary/derivative publication)
- **Target Audience:** Family caregivers of persons with Alzheimer's disease or related dementias
- **Study Design:** Multisite randomized controlled trial (RCT)
- **Funding:** National Institute on Aging (NIA), National Institute of Nursing Research (NINR)
- **Significance:** One of the largest and most rigorous RCTs of caregiver interventions ever conducted; designated an evidence-based program by SAMHSA's National Registry of Evidence-Based Programs and Practices (NREPP) and the ACL/AoA

### 3.2 Program History and Development

REACH II built on the original REACH I study (1995-2001), which tested multiple different intervention approaches across six sites. REACH I demonstrated that multicomponent caregiver interventions were feasible and showed promise. REACH II (2001-2006) then tested a single, standardized multicomponent intervention protocol across five sites with a diverse sample including White/Caucasian, African American/Black, and Hispanic/Latino caregivers. The program was specifically designed to address health disparities in caregiver support by ensuring racial and ethnic diversity.

### 3.3 Intervention Structure

The REACH II intervention was delivered over **6 months** and included:

- **12 individual in-home sessions** (approximately 1-1.5 hours each)
- **5 telephone support sessions**
- **Total contact:** Approximately 12 in-person visits + 5 phone calls over 6 months
- **Delivery:** Trained interventionists (typically master's-level clinicians or trained bachelor's-level staff)
- **Tailored approach:** While the protocol was standardized, the specific content emphasis within sessions was tailored based on an individualized risk assessment of each caregiver's needs

### 3.4 Risk Assessment Framework

A key innovation of REACH II was the use of a structured **risk assessment** at baseline and throughout the intervention to identify each caregiver's specific risk areas. Five risk domains were assessed:

1. **Depression** -- Caregiver depressive symptoms
2. **Burden** -- Subjective feelings of being overwhelmed
3. **Self-care and healthy behaviors** -- Physical health, healthcare utilization, health behaviors
4. **Social support** -- Size and quality of support network
5. **Problem behaviors** -- Care recipient behavioral and psychological symptoms (BPSD)

The risk assessment guided which intervention components received the most emphasis for each caregiver-care recipient dyad.

### 3.5 Intervention Components and Topics

The REACH II intervention addressed five major content areas, each with specific strategies and skills:

#### Component 1: Safety
- Home safety assessment and modification
- Fall prevention strategies
- Wandering prevention and management
- Emergency preparedness
- Driving safety and driving cessation
- Medication safety
- Firearms and weapons safety (relevant given diverse geographic sites)
- Environmental hazard identification and remediation

#### Component 2: Social Support
- Enhancing caregiver social support networks
- Strategies for asking for and accepting help
- Family communication and conflict resolution
- Building and maintaining support networks
- Managing social isolation
- Connecting with community resources and support groups
- Faith-based and spiritual community connections
- Respite care education and utilization

#### Component 3: Problem Behaviors (BPSD Management)
- Understanding behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)
- The ABC (Antecedent-Behavior-Consequence) framework for analyzing behaviors
- Identifying triggers for behavioral symptoms
- Specific strategies for common behaviors:
  - Agitation and aggressive behavior
  - Wandering and exit-seeking
  - Sundowning and sleep disturbances
  - Repetitive questioning and behaviors
  - Resistance to care (bathing, dressing, eating)
  - Hallucinations and delusions
  - Inappropriate sexual behavior
  - Hoarding and hiding objects
- Communication techniques for persons with dementia
- Redirection and distraction strategies
- Activity planning and engagement
- Environmental modifications to reduce behavioral triggers
- When to seek professional help for behavioral symptoms
- Medication awareness (understanding that behaviors may indicate pain, infection, or medication side effects)

#### Component 4: Depression and Emotional Well-Being
- Understanding caregiver depression and its impact
- Cognitive-behavioral strategies for managing negative thoughts
- Pleasant events scheduling (increasing positive activities)
- Relaxation training (deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation)
- Stress management techniques
- Grief and loss in caregiving (ambiguous loss)
- Managing anger and frustration
- Self-compassion and emotional acceptance
- When to seek professional mental health support
- Culturally appropriate coping strategies

#### Component 5: Self-Care and Health Behaviors
- Caregiver physical health monitoring and maintenance
- Exercise and physical activity for caregivers
- Nutrition and healthy eating for caregivers
- Sleep hygiene and managing sleep disruption
- Healthcare utilization (encouraging caregivers to attend their own medical appointments)
- Medication management for caregivers' own health
- Respite care planning and utilization
- Time management and priority setting
- Setting boundaries and asking for help
- Long-term care planning and decision-making

### 3.6 Additional Intervention Elements

- **Technology component:** Provision of a telephone-linked care system for some participants; use of structured telephone support
- **Written materials:** Caregiver handbook and tip sheets provided
- **Notebook/binder:** Caregivers received a structured notebook to organize information, track behaviors, and record strategies
- **Culturally tailored content:** Materials were adapted for African American, Hispanic/Latino, and White/Caucasian caregivers, including language-appropriate materials (English and Spanish)
- **Community resource referral:** Interventionists connected caregivers with local services (adult day care, respite, support groups, Alzheimer's Association chapters)

### 3.7 Key Outcomes and Evidence

The REACH II trial demonstrated statistically significant improvements in:

- Overall quality of life for caregivers (primary outcome)
- Caregiver depression symptoms
- Caregiver burden
- Care recipient problem behaviors (as reported by caregivers)
- Social support
- Caregiver self-care behaviors
- These effects were observed across all three racial/ethnic groups

The program was subsequently translated for community-based delivery as **REACH OUT** and **REACH VA** (adapted for the Veterans Administration healthcare system), demonstrating that the intervention could be implemented outside of research settings.

  - NIA ADEAR Center: https://www.nia.nih.gov/alzheimers
  - Rosalynn Carter Institute for Caregivers (has implemented REACH-based programs)

**Coverage Assessment:** REACH II maps primarily to **5 of 16 domains** (Domains 2, 3, 12, 13, 16), with the deepest coverage in Domain 2 (Dementia Care) and Domain 12 (Caregiver Self-Care). REACH II does not address ADLs/personal care skills, clinical conditions (Tier 2), infection control, nutrition skills, or medication management in the way that HHA training curricula do. This is expected -- REACH II targets family caregivers, not professional HHAs, and focuses on psychosocial support rather than hands-on care skills.

## 4. SAVVY Caregiver Program

### 4.1 Program Overview

- **Full Name:** The Savvy Caregiver Program
- **Developer:** Kenneth Hepburn, PhD (University of Minnesota, later Emory University)
- **Co-developers:** Marsha Lewis, PhD; Lore Wright, PhD; Lisa P. Gwyther, MSW (additional contributors)
- **Original Institution:** University of Minnesota School of Nursing
- **Current Status:** Evidence-based program listed in multiple registries including the ACL/AoA National Registry of Evidence-based Programs, the Benjamin Rose Institute on Aging (BRI) listings, and the Rosalynn Carter Institute program catalogue
- **Target Audience:** Family caregivers of persons with Alzheimer's disease or related dementias (primarily community-dwelling)
- **Format:** Group-based psychoeducational program
- **Designation:** Listed by the Administration for Community Living (ACL) as an evidence-based caregiver support program eligible for Older Americans Act Title III-E funding

### 4.2 Program Structure

- **Duration:** 6 sessions over 6 weeks (some implementations use a 7-session format)
- **Session length:** 2 hours per session
- **Total hours:** 12-14 hours of structured content
- **Group size:** Typically 8-12 caregivers per group
- **Facilitation:** Trained group facilitators (social workers, nurses, health educators, or trained community program staff)
- **Materials:** Caregiver handbook/manual, worksheets, activity planning guides
- **Setting:** Community-based settings (senior centers, churches, Area Agencies on Aging, healthcare facilities, virtual/online adaptations)

### 4.3 Theoretical Framework

The Savvy Caregiver Program is grounded in:

- **Stress-process model** (Pearlin et al.) -- Understanding caregiving as a process with primary stressors (care demands), secondary stressors (role strain, family conflict), and mediators (coping, social support)
- **Self-efficacy theory** (Bandura) -- Building caregivers' confidence in their ability to manage caregiving challenges
- **Competence-based approach** -- Framing caregiving as a role that requires learnable skills, not innate ability
- **Person-centered dementia care** (Kitwood) -- Understanding the person with dementia as a whole person with preserved capacities, not just deficits

### 4.4 Session-by-Session Curriculum

#### Session 1: Understanding the Dementia Experience
- What is dementia? (Types: Alzheimer's, vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal)
- How dementia affects the brain: the biology of cognitive decline
- Understanding preserved vs. lost capacities at different stages
- The concept of "contented involvement" -- maintaining quality of life through meaningful engagement
- Understanding the caregiver role as a skilled activity
- Introduction to the "caregiving as performance" metaphor
- Setting realistic expectations for the program

#### Session 2: Getting into Your Loved One's World
- Understanding the subjective experience of living with dementia
- Seeing the world from the care recipient's perspective
- Assessing the person's current abilities and limitations (functional assessment)
- Identifying remaining strengths and interests
- The "Savvy Caregiver" approach: working WITH remaining abilities rather than against deficits
- Behavioral observation skills: what to look for and how to interpret behavior
- Understanding behavior as communication of needs

#### Session 3: Communication
- How dementia affects communication (expressive and receptive language)
- Adapting communication to the person's cognitive level
- Simplifying language: short sentences, one-step instructions
- Using non-verbal communication effectively (tone, gesture, facial expression, touch)
- Reducing communication barriers (noise, distraction, overstimulation)
- Validation and empathetic communication
- Techniques for difficult conversations
- Avoiding arguing, correcting, or quizzing

#### Session 4: Managing Daily Life and Activities
- Structuring the day for a person with dementia (routines, predictability)
- Adapting activities of daily living (bathing, dressing, eating, grooming)
- Creating meaningful activities matched to the person's abilities
- The activity hierarchy: simplifying, cueing, assisting, hands-on
- Managing transitions (between activities, locations, times of day)
- Environmental modifications to support independence
- Managing mealtime challenges
- Sleep and nighttime management

#### Session 5: Managing Challenging Behaviors
- Why people with dementia exhibit challenging behaviors (unmet needs model)
- Common behavioral challenges: agitation, resistance to care, repetition, wandering, sundowning, aggression
- The "detective" approach: investigating triggers and patterns
- Behavioral management strategies:
  - Addressing underlying needs (pain, hunger, toileting, boredom, fear)
  - Environmental modification (reducing stimulation, improving lighting)
  - Redirection and distraction techniques
  - Structured routines to reduce confusion
  - Responding to hallucinations and delusions
- De-escalation techniques
- When behavior requires professional consultation
- Medication awareness: what families should know about psychotropic medications

#### Session 6: Taking Care of Yourself
- The stress of caregiving: understanding caregiver burden and burnout
- Recognizing signs of caregiver stress and depression
- Self-care strategies: physical health, emotional well-being, social connections
- Managing grief and loss (living with ambiguous loss)
- Setting limits and asking for help
- Building and using a support network
- Respite care: types, benefits, overcoming barriers
- Community resources: Alzheimer's Association, Area Agencies on Aging, support groups
- Planning for the future: advance directives, long-term care options, financial and legal planning
- Developing a personal caregiver action plan

#### Optional Session 7 (in some implementations): Putting It All Together
- Review of key concepts and skills
- Caregiver action plan development and sharing
- Problem-solving practice with case scenarios
- Ongoing resource identification
- Graduation and celebration
- Follow-up and booster session planning

### 4.5 Key Competencies Taught to Caregivers

| Competency Area | Specific Skills |
|---|---|
| Dementia knowledge | Disease understanding, stages, preserved capacities, prognosis |
| Assessment skills | Observing and assessing functional abilities, behavioral patterns |
| Communication | Adapting speech, non-verbal techniques, validation, avoiding confrontation |
| Activity planning | Matching activities to abilities, structuring routines, creating meaningful engagement |
| Behavioral management | Trigger identification, needs-based approach, redirection, de-escalation |
| Self-care | Stress recognition, self-care planning, respite utilization, support seeking |
| Problem-solving | Detective approach to behaviors, individualized strategy development |
| Care planning | Future planning, advance directives, long-term care decision-making |

### 4.6 Evidence Base

The Savvy Caregiver Program has been evaluated in multiple studies:

- **Original RCT (Hepburn et al., 2001):** Demonstrated improvements in caregiver mastery, competence, and depression; reductions in care-related strain
- **Replications:** Multiple studies have replicated positive outcomes across different settings and populations
- **Cultural adaptations:** The program has been adapted for Native American caregivers (Savvy Caregiver for Native Americans), rural caregivers, and online delivery (Tele-Savvy)
- **Tele-Savvy:** A technology-adapted version delivered via videoconference, developed by Kenneth Hepburn at Emory University, which showed comparable outcomes to in-person delivery
- **Key publications:**
  - Hepburn KW, Tornatore J, Center B, Ostwald SW. Dementia family caregiver training: affecting beliefs about caregiving and caregiver outcomes. J Am Geriatr Soc. 2001;49(4):450-457.
  - Hepburn KW, Lewis M, Tornatore J, Sherman CW, Bremer KL. The Savvy Caregiver program: the demonstrated effectiveness of a transportable dementia caregiver psychoeducation program. J Gerontol Nurs. 2007;33(3):30-36.

### 4.7 Availability and Access

- **Access:** Program materials are available for community organizations through a **training-of-trainers** model
- **Cost:** Facilitator training is required; costs vary by training provider and format
- **Licensing:** Organizations must complete facilitator training to implement the program with fidelity
- **Training providers:** Multiple organizations offer Savvy Caregiver facilitator training, including:
  - Rosalynn Carter Institute for Caregivers (https://www.rosalynncarter.org/)
  - Area Agencies on Aging (many have trained facilitators)
  - Alzheimer's Association chapters (some offer or refer to Savvy)
- **Funding eligibility:** Eligible for Older Americans Act Title III-E National Family Caregiver Support Program funding
- **Tele-Savvy:** Online version available; information at Emory University

Unlike REACH II, Savvy Caregiver provides more structured psychoeducation about dementia itself (disease understanding, stages, brain changes) and focuses heavily on building caregiving competence through knowledge. It does not address infection control, medication management, nutrition skills, clinical conditions beyond dementia, or wound/skin care.

### 5.3 Complementarity Analysis

The four programs are **highly complementary** and serve different but overlapping needs:

1. **PHI + NYS DOH** provide the **technical/clinical caregiving skills** foundation (ADLs, vital signs, infection control, safety, clinical conditions). These are the "hard skills" of caregiving.

2. **REACH II + SAVVY** provide the **psychosocial/behavioral skills** foundation (dementia behavior management, emotional coping, social support, self-care). These are the "soft skills" and coping resources.

3. **Together**, all four programs cover **all 16 domains** of the CareExpert AI taxonomy.

4. **The gap** that none fully addresses is **medication management** (Domain 4), which is intentionally limited across all programs due to scope of practice restrictions. CareExpert AI content for this domain must draw primarily from clinical guidelines (CMS, pharmacy sources) rather than training curricula.

## 6. Implications for CareExpert AI

### 6.1 Content Architecture Recommendations

Based on the analysis of these four foundational curricula, the CareExpert AI knowledge base should:

1. **Use PHI/NYS DOH content as the backbone** for Tier 1 (ADLs, Safety, Escalation) and Tier 2 (Clinical Conditions) domains, as these provide the most comprehensive hands-on caregiving guidance.

2. **Use REACH II content as the backbone** for Domain 12 (Caregiver Self-Care) and the psychosocial components of Domain 2 (Dementia Care), particularly behavioral management strategies, emotional coping, and social support.

3. **Use SAVVY Caregiver content as the backbone** for Domain 2 (Dementia Care) psychoeducation components, particularly understanding dementia, communication techniques, and activity planning.

4. **Integrate across all four sources** for Domain 13 (Communication Skills) and Domain 2 (Behavioral Management), where each program contributes unique perspectives and strategies.

### 6.2 Pedagogical Design

The curricula collectively suggest that CareExpert AI should:

- Use **scenario-based, problem-solving** approaches (consistent with PHI's coaching model)
- Provide **individualized, risk-assessed** responses (consistent with REACH II's tailored approach)
- Frame caregiving as a **learnable skill** requiring knowledge and practice (consistent with SAVVY's competence model)
- Include both **practical "how-to" guidance** (from PHI/NYS DOH) and **emotional/coping support** (from REACH II/SAVVY)
- Offer **culturally responsive** content (emphasized by all four programs)

### 6.3 Key Content Gaps to Address from Other Sources

| Gap Area | Recommended Sources |
|---|---|
| Medication management details | CMS CoPs, pharmacy guidelines, FDA resources |
| Condition-specific clinical guidance (beyond curricula depth) | NIA, AHA, ADA, Alzheimer's Association clinical guidelines |
| Legal/regulatory specifics by state | State nurse practice acts, state HHA registries |
| Technology-assisted caregiving | Emerging literature, AHRQ, NIA-funded technology studies |
| Pediatric and maternal care | NYS DOH Unit 13, specialized pediatric home care curricula |
| Advanced wound care | NPUAP/EPUAP guidelines, wound care specialty resources |

### 6.4 Licensing and Attribution Considerations

| Program | Can Content Be Freely Used? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| PHI HHA Curriculum | NO -- Commercial product | Must purchase curriculum; cannot reproduce without license. Can reference structure and topics. |
| NYS DOH Curriculum | YES -- Public regulatory standard | State regulatory framework is public; can reference freely. Specific training materials may vary. |
| REACH II | YES -- Public domain (federally funded) | Intervention protocols and manuals available; federally funded research is in the public domain. |
| SAVVY Caregiver | LIMITED -- Training-of-trainers model | Program concepts and general approach can be referenced; specific materials require facilitator training/license. |

**Recommendation:** CareExpert AI should draw on the **principles, competencies, and topic structures** from all four programs while ensuring that specific proprietary content (particularly from PHI and SAVVY) is not reproduced verbatim. REACH II materials, as federally funded, provide the most freely usable detailed content.
