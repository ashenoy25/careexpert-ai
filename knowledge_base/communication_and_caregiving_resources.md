## 1. Caregiver Communication with Healthcare Providers

### Overview

Effective communication between caregivers and healthcare providers is essential for ensuring high-quality care, preventing medical errors, and advocating for the care recipient's needs. Family caregivers serve as a critical link between the patient and the healthcare team, providing information that clinicians may not otherwise have access to and implementing care plans at home.

### NIA Resources on Doctor-Patient Communication

**Source URL:** https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/doctor-patient-communication
**Key Topics:**
- Preparing for medical appointments
- Asking the right questions
- Understanding medical information
- Communicating concerns effectively
- Following up on test results and treatment changes

### Preparing for Medical Appointments

**Before the Visit:**
- Write down all questions and concerns in advance; prioritize the most important ones
- Prepare a current medication list (prescription, over-the-counter, supplements, vitamins) with dosages and frequency
- Document any new symptoms, changes in condition, or concerns since the last visit
- Bring relevant medical records, test results, or reports from other providers
- Note any changes in daily functioning (eating, sleeping, mobility, behavior, cognition)
- If the care recipient has difficulty communicating, prepare to describe symptoms and observations on their behalf
- Consider bringing a notebook or using a phone to take notes during the visit
- Bring insurance cards and a list of all healthcare providers involved in care

**During the Visit:**
- Start with the most important concern first
- Be specific about symptoms: when they started, how often they occur, what makes them better or worse, how they affect daily functioning
- Ask for clarification if you do not understand something -- there is no such thing as a silly question
- Use the "teach-back" method: repeat back what the provider said in your own words to confirm understanding
- Ask about the purpose, risks, and benefits of any proposed tests, medications, or treatments
- Ask about expected outcomes and what to watch for
- Request written instructions whenever possible
- Ask who to call if problems arise or questions come up later

**After the Visit:**
- Review notes and instructions; clarify anything unclear as soon as possible
- Fill prescriptions promptly and understand new medication instructions
- Follow up on referrals and scheduled tests
- Implement any changes to the care plan
- Keep a record of the visit summary, any new diagnoses, medication changes, and follow-up plans
- Share relevant information with other caregivers or family members involved in care

### Key Questions for Caregivers to Ask Healthcare Providers

**About Diagnoses and Conditions:**
- What is the diagnosis and what does it mean?
- What caused this condition?
- How will this condition change over time?
- What symptoms should I watch for?
- When should I call the office or go to the emergency room?

**About Medications:**
- What is this medication for?
- How should it be taken (timing, with or without food, what to avoid)?
- What are the possible side effects?
- How long should it be taken?
- How will I know if it is working?
- Does it interact with any other medications or supplements?
- What happens if a dose is missed?

**About Tests and Procedures:**
- What is the purpose of this test/procedure?
- What do the results mean?
- Are there any risks involved?
- How should I prepare the care recipient?
- When and how will we get the results?

**About Care Planning:**
- What can I do at home to help manage this condition?
- Are there any activity, dietary, or lifestyle changes needed?
- What home health services or community resources are available?
- Should we consider hospice or palliative care at this point?
- What should we expect going forward?

## 2. SBAR Communication Tool for Home Health Care

### Overview

SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) is a structured communication tool originally developed by the U.S. Navy and adapted for healthcare by Kaiser Permanente. It provides a standardized framework for communicating critical information clearly, concisely, and completely. While originally designed for clinical professionals, SBAR has been adapted for use by family caregivers and home health aides to communicate with healthcare providers.

### The SBAR Framework

| Component | What It Covers | What to Communicate |
|-----------|---------------|---------------------|
| **S - Situation** | What is happening right now? | The current problem or concern; what prompted the communication |
| **B - Background** | What is the relevant context? | Pertinent medical history, current diagnoses, medications, recent changes, baseline status |
| **A - Assessment** | What do you think is going on? | Your observations and evaluation of the situation; how the person looks, feels, and is functioning compared to their baseline |
| **R - Recommendation** | What do you think needs to happen? | What action you are requesting or what you think should be done next |

### SBAR Examples for Caregivers

#### Example 1: Reporting a Fever

| Component | Communication |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Situation** | "I am calling about [patient name]. They have a temperature of 101.5 degrees F. This started about 2 hours ago." |
| **Background** | "They have a history of COPD and diabetes. They completed a course of antibiotics for a UTI last week. Their medications include metformin, lisinopril, and albuterol. They have been eating and drinking less than usual for the past day." |
| **Assessment** | "I am concerned because the fever is new, they seem more confused than usual, and they just finished antibiotics. I am worried the infection may not have fully resolved or there may be a new infection." |
| **Recommendation** | "I would like the nurse or doctor to evaluate them. Should I bring them in, or can someone come to the home? Do you want me to give acetaminophen in the meantime?" |

#### Example 2: Reporting a Fall

| Component | Communication |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Situation** | "I am calling about [patient name]. They fell in the bathroom about 30 minutes ago while trying to get to the toilet. They are complaining of pain in their right hip." |
| **Background** | "They are 82 years old with osteoporosis and Parkinson's disease. They use a walker but tried to go to the bathroom without it. They take carbidopa-levodopa, alendronate, and calcium with vitamin D. They fell once before about three months ago without injury." |
| **Assessment** | "They are alert and oriented but in significant pain. They cannot bear weight on the right leg. I am worried they may have fractured their hip." |
| **Recommendation** | "I think they need to be evaluated in the emergency department. Should I call 911 or is there another way to transport them safely?" |

#### Example 3: Reporting Behavioral Changes in Dementia

| Component | Communication |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Situation** | "I am calling about [patient name]. Over the past two days, they have become significantly more agitated and confused. They are pacing, refusing to eat, and did not sleep last night." |
| **Background** | "They have moderate Alzheimer's disease and usually have good days and bad days, but this is different from their normal pattern. Their current medications are donepezil and memantine. No recent medication changes. They have not had a bowel movement in three days." |
| **Assessment** | "I am concerned that something is causing this change -- possibly pain, constipation, a urinary tract infection, or a medication issue. This is not their usual behavior pattern." |
| **Recommendation** | "I think they need to be evaluated for a medical cause of the behavioral change. Can they be seen today? Should I check for signs of a UTI or try a laxative for the constipation in the meantime?" |

### Adapting SBAR for Non-Clinical Caregivers

**Key Principles:**
- You do not need to use medical terminology; describe what you see and observe in plain language
- Be as specific as possible: use numbers (temperature, blood pressure, how many times), times, and descriptions
- Compare the current situation to the person's baseline ("This is different from their usual...")
- Trust your instincts -- you know the person best and are most likely to notice subtle changes
- Write down your SBAR before calling so you communicate clearly and do not forget important details
- Keep an SBAR worksheet by the phone or on the refrigerator for easy access during an urgent situation

**SBAR Worksheet Template for Caregivers:**

```
SITUATION: What is happening?
- Patient name: _______________
- What is the problem? _______________
- When did it start? _______________
- How severe is it (1-10)? _______________

BACKGROUND: What is the relevant history?
- Diagnosis(es): _______________
- Current medications: _______________
- Allergies: _______________
- Recent changes (new medications, falls, hospitalizations): _______________
- Baseline status (how are they usually?): _______________

ASSESSMENT: What do I think is going on?
- What have I noticed that is different? _______________
- What am I most concerned about? _______________
- Vital signs (if available): Temp ___ BP ___ Pulse ___ O2 sat ___

RECOMMENDATION: What do I think needs to happen?
- What am I requesting? _______________
- How urgent is this? _______________
- Is there anything I should do while waiting? _______________
```

### Benefits of Using SBAR in Home Care

- Provides structure to communication, especially during stressful or urgent situations
- Ensures critical information is not forgotten or omitted
- Enables concise, focused communication that respects healthcare providers' time
- Reduces miscommunication and misunderstandings
- Empowers caregivers to participate actively in the care team
- Creates a shared language between clinical and non-clinical members of the care team
- Improves patient safety by ensuring timely and complete reporting of changes

## 3. Reporting Changes in Condition

### Why Reporting Changes Matters

Older adults, especially those with chronic conditions or cognitive impairment, may not be able to identify or communicate changes in their health. Caregivers are often the first to notice subtle changes that may indicate a new problem, worsening of an existing condition, medication side effects, or a medical emergency. Timely reporting of changes can prevent hospitalizations, complications, and adverse outcomes.

### Common Changes Caregivers Should Report

#### Changes Requiring Immediate Attention (Call 911 or Go to ER)

- Sudden difficulty breathing or shortness of breath
- Chest pain or pressure
- Signs of stroke (sudden numbness/weakness on one side, sudden confusion, trouble speaking, sudden severe headache, sudden vision changes) -- use FAST: Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call 911
- Unresponsiveness or loss of consciousness
- Severe bleeding that does not stop with direct pressure
- Seizures (if not typical for the person)
- Severe allergic reaction (difficulty breathing, swelling of face/throat, hives)
- Suspected poisoning or overdose
- Severe injury from a fall (possible fracture, head injury)

#### Changes to Report Same Day (Call the Healthcare Provider)

- New or worsening pain
- Fever (temperature 100.4 degrees F or higher, or as directed by the provider)
- Falls (even without apparent injury)
- Sudden confusion or change in mental status (especially if different from baseline)
- New or worsening swelling in legs, ankles, or feet
- Sudden weight gain (2-3 pounds in a day or 5 pounds in a week -- may indicate fluid retention)
- Difficulty breathing with activity (new or worsening)
- New or worsening cough
- Vomiting or diarrhea lasting more than 24 hours
- Inability to eat or drink for more than 24 hours
- New skin rash, redness, or signs of infection (warmth, redness, swelling, drainage)
- Significant change in behavior or mood
- Signs of urinary tract infection (burning, frequency, urgency, cloudy or foul-smelling urine, new confusion)
- Medication reactions (new symptoms after starting a medication)
- Blood in urine, stool, or vomit

#### Changes to Report at the Next Scheduled Visit

- Gradual changes in appetite, weight, or energy level
- Increasing difficulty with activities of daily living
- Gradual changes in mood or social engagement
- New or worsening constipation or incontinence
- Increasing difficulty with memory or cognition
- Concerns about medication effectiveness
- Changes in sleep patterns
- Skin changes (new bruises, dryness, pressure marks)
- Concerns about safety at home

### How to Report Changes Effectively

- Use the SBAR framework (see Section 2)
- Be specific: describe exactly what you observed, when it started, and how it compares to baseline
- Have the medication list and medical history available when calling
- If calling after hours, have the care recipient's chart number or date of birth available
- Document the change: write down what you observed, when you reported it, who you spoke to, and what instructions were given
- Follow through on instructions and report back if the situation does not improve

### Creating a Caregiver Communication Log

Maintaining a daily or weekly log helps track changes over time and provides objective data for healthcare providers.

**Suggested Log Format:**

| Date/Time | Observation | Action Taken | Provider Contacted | Instructions Received |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|-------------------|----------------------|
| | | | | |

**What to Track:**
- Vital signs (if monitoring: blood pressure, temperature, pulse, weight, blood sugar)
- Food and fluid intake
- Bowel and bladder function
- Sleep quality and duration
- Pain level (0-10 scale) and location
- Mood and behavior
- Activity level and mobility
- Medications given (time, dose, any missed doses)
- Falls or near-falls
- Skin condition (any new redness, wounds, or concerns)
- Any unusual observations

## 4. Communicating with Families

### Overview

When multiple family members are involved in a care recipient's life, clear communication among family members is essential to ensure consistent, coordinated care and to prevent conflict. Caregiving often brings existing family dynamics and tensions to the surface, making intentional communication strategies especially important.

### Family Care Meetings

**Purpose:**
- Share information about the care recipient's current condition and needs
- Discuss care responsibilities and divide tasks
- Address concerns, conflicts, or disagreements
- Make decisions about care options (home care, facility placement, hospice)
- Review advance directives and end-of-life preferences
- Support each other emotionally

**How to Organize a Family Care Meeting:**
- Include all family members who are involved in or affected by caregiving decisions (in person, by phone, or by video)
- Set an agenda in advance and share it with all participants
- Choose a neutral facilitator (a family member, social worker, clergy member, or mediator)
- Establish ground rules: listen respectfully, speak one at a time, focus on solutions, avoid blame
- Start with an update on the care recipient's current status
- Discuss each agenda item and work toward decisions
- Assign specific tasks and responsibilities with clear timelines
- Schedule the next meeting
- Share a written summary of decisions and assignments with all participants

**Topics to Address:**
- Medical updates and prognosis
- Current care plan and any changes needed
- Division of caregiving responsibilities (daily care, transportation, finances, respite coverage)
- Financial issues (cost of care, insurance, benefits, shared expenses)
- Legal matters (power of attorney, advance directives, estate planning)
- Emotional support for the primary caregiver
- Long-term planning (what happens when needs increase?)
- Disagreements about care decisions

### Managing Family Conflict in Caregiving

**Common Sources of Conflict:**
- Unequal distribution of caregiving responsibilities
- Disagreements about the care recipient's needs or best course of action
- Financial disputes
- Differing opinions about end-of-life care
- Old family dynamics and unresolved issues resurfacing
- Geographical distance (long-distance family members may not understand day-to-day demands)
- Cultural differences in expectations about caregiving roles

**Conflict Resolution Strategies:**
- Focus on the care recipient's needs and wishes, not personal agendas
- Acknowledge that everyone is doing their best within their circumstances
- Use "I" statements rather than "you" statements ("I feel overwhelmed" vs. "You never help")
- Seek to understand different perspectives before trying to be understood
- Consider involving a professional mediator or family counselor if conflicts persist
- Recognize that not all family members can contribute equally; identify what each person can realistically offer (time, money, emotional support, long-distance coordination)
- Document decisions and agreements to prevent misunderstandings
- Accept that some disagreements may not be fully resolved; focus on what can be agreed upon

### Long-Distance Caregiving Communication

- Schedule regular check-in calls or video calls with the primary caregiver and care recipient
- Use shared digital tools (calendars, care coordination apps such as CaringBridge, CareZone, or Lotsa Helping Hands) to stay updated
- Offer specific help rather than general offers ("I can handle the insurance paperwork" vs. "Let me know if you need anything")
- Plan visits strategically to give the primary caregiver a break
- Respect the primary caregiver's judgment on daily care decisions
- Contribute financially if possible

## 5. Cultural Sensitivity in Caregiving Communication

### Overview

Cultural beliefs, values, and practices significantly influence how families experience illness, caregiving, and end-of-life decisions. Effective caregiving communication requires awareness of and respect for cultural differences. Cultural sensitivity is not about knowing every detail of every culture, but about approaching each person and family with openness, humility, and a willingness to learn.

### Key Dimensions of Cultural Influence on Caregiving

**Health Beliefs and Practices:**
- Different cultures have varying beliefs about the causes of illness (biomedical, spiritual, social, environmental)
- Some cultures use traditional or complementary healing practices alongside or instead of Western medicine
- Attitudes toward medication, surgery, and other interventions may vary
- The concept of mental health and acceptability of mental health treatment differs across cultures

**Family Roles and Decision-Making:**
- In some cultures, the eldest son, eldest daughter, or patriarch makes healthcare decisions
- In others, decisions are made collectively by the family
- Gender roles in caregiving vary significantly across cultures
- The concept of individual autonomy vs. family-centered decision-making affects advance care planning

**Communication Styles:**
- Direct vs. indirect communication styles (some cultures value direct honesty; others consider it impolite)
- Disclosure of terminal diagnoses: some families prefer that the patient not be told of a terminal prognosis
- Eye contact, personal space, and touch have different meanings across cultures
- Language barriers can significantly impair communication and quality of care; interpreter services should be used rather than family members (especially children) as interpreters

**End-of-Life and Bereavement Practices:**
- Attitudes toward hospice, palliative care, and do-not-resuscitate orders vary significantly
- Specific rituals around death and dying (last rites, bathing the body, keeping vigil, specific prayers)
- Mourning practices and periods (shiva, 40-day mourning periods, wake traditions)
- Attitudes toward organ donation, autopsy, and cremation vs. burial

**Diet and Nutrition:**
- Religious dietary laws (halal, kosher, vegetarian, fasting practices)
- Traditional foods and their significance in health and healing
- Beliefs about "hot" and "cold" foods in illness
- Food as an expression of love and care -- may complicate nutritional restrictions

### Principles of Culturally Sensitive Communication

1. **Cultural humility:** Approach each interaction with curiosity and a willingness to learn; avoid assumptions based on a person's apparent cultural background
2. **Ask, don't assume:** "Can you tell me about any cultural or religious practices that are important to you during this time?"
3. **Use professional interpreters:** When language barriers exist, use trained medical interpreters (in person or by phone/video); avoid using family members as interpreters for medical discussions
4. **Respect family dynamics:** Understand who the decision-makers are and involve them appropriately
5. **Adapt communication style:** Match the family's preferred level of directness, formality, and information disclosure
6. **Integrate traditional practices:** When safe, incorporate traditional healing practices alongside medical care
7. **Address spiritual needs:** Connect patients and families with chaplains, spiritual advisors, or community leaders who share their cultural background
8. **Recognize your own biases:** Everyone has cultural blind spots; ongoing self-reflection is essential
9. **Avoid stereotyping:** Cultural tendencies are generalizations; each individual and family is unique
10. **Document cultural preferences:** Include cultural and spiritual preferences in the care plan so all team members are aware

### Practical Guidance for Caregivers

- If you are a family caregiver from a different cultural background than the care recipient (in-law caregiving, intercultural marriages), seek to understand the care recipient's cultural values and preferences
- If you are hiring professional caregivers, discuss the care recipient's cultural preferences regarding food, personal care, communication, and spiritual practices
- Share cultural information with healthcare providers to ensure care plans are culturally appropriate
- Seek community organizations that serve the care recipient's cultural or ethnic group for additional support and resources

## 6. Communication with Persons Living with Dementia

### Overview

As dementia progresses, communication becomes increasingly challenging. The person may have difficulty finding words, understanding complex sentences, following conversations, or expressing needs. Effective communication strategies can reduce frustration, maintain connection, and preserve dignity.

### NIA and Alzheimer's Association Communication Guidelines

**Source URLs:**
- https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-and-dementia/communicating-person-who-has-alzheimers-disease
- https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/communications

### Communication Strategies by Stage

#### Early Stage (Mild Dementia)
- The person can still participate in meaningful conversations
- Allow extra time for responses; do not finish sentences for them unless asked
- Include the person in decisions about their care and future planning
- Be patient with repetition; respond as if hearing the information for the first time
- Reduce background noise during conversations
- Use clear, simple language but do not talk down to the person
- Maintain normal social interactions and activities

#### Middle Stage (Moderate Dementia)
- Use short, simple sentences with one idea at a time
- Give step-by-step instructions (one step at a time, not a sequence)
- Use visual cues, gestures, and demonstrations to supplement verbal communication
- Offer choices with only two options (hold up two shirts rather than asking an open-ended question)
- Use a calm, reassuring tone of voice
- Make eye contact and approach from the front
- Avoid arguing, correcting, or quizzing the person
- Redirect the conversation or activity when the person becomes frustrated or agitated
- Label drawers, cabinets, and rooms with words or pictures
- Use familiar names and references

#### Late Stage (Severe Dementia)
- The person may communicate primarily through facial expressions, gestures, sounds, and touch
- Continue to speak to the person with respect and warmth, even if they do not appear to respond
- Use a gentle tone of voice and reassuring touch
- Pay attention to nonverbal cues: facial expressions, body language, agitation, crying
- Hearing is believed to be present even when the person appears unresponsive
- Play familiar music, read aloud, or sit quietly holding the person's hand
- Assume the person can understand more than they can express

### General Communication Principles for All Stages

- **Approach from the front** and identify yourself by name
- **Use the person's preferred name**; maintain respect and dignity
- **Minimize distractions** (turn off TV, reduce background noise)
- **Be patient**; allow ample time for the person to process and respond
- **Listen actively**; focus on the feelings behind the words, not just the words themselves
- **Validate emotions** rather than correcting facts ("You miss your mother" rather than "Your mother died 20 years ago")
- **Use a warm, calm tone** of voice; avoid raising your voice
- **Avoid abstract language**, sarcasm, idioms, and humor that may confuse
- **Offer reassurance** through words, tone, and gentle touch
- **Do not take difficult behaviors personally**; they are symptoms of the disease, not intentional acts

## 7. Health Literacy and Clear Communication

### Overview

Health literacy is the degree to which individuals have the ability to find, understand, and use information and services to inform health-related decisions and actions for themselves and others. An estimated 36% of U.S. adults have basic or below-basic health literacy, and rates are higher among older adults, non-native English speakers, and those with lower educational attainment. Low health literacy is associated with poorer health outcomes, higher hospitalization rates, and difficulty managing chronic conditions.

### Plain Language Principles for Caregiver Communication

**When creating written materials or communicating health information to caregivers:**

- **Use everyday language:** Replace medical jargon with common words ("high blood pressure" instead of "hypertension," "sugar disease" instead of "diabetes")
- **Keep sentences short:** Aim for one idea per sentence
- **Use active voice:** "Take this medication with food" instead of "This medication should be taken with food"
- **Be specific:** "Take 2 pills at breakfast" instead of "Take twice daily"
- **Use numbers carefully:** Write out "two pills" rather than "2 pills" when possible; avoid fractions or decimals when possible
- **Organize information logically:** Put the most important information first
- **Use headers, bullets, and white space** to make documents easy to scan
- **Include pictures or diagrams** when possible to reinforce written instructions
- **Use the teach-back method:** Ask the person to explain what they understood in their own words

### Teach-Back Method

The teach-back (or show-me) method is an evidence-based communication technique that confirms understanding. Rather than asking "Do you understand?" (which most people will answer yes to regardless), it asks the person to explain the information in their own words.

**How to use teach-back:**
1. Explain the information clearly
2. Say: "I want to make sure I explained this clearly. Can you tell me in your own words what you will do when you get home?"
3. If the person cannot accurately explain, clarify the information and try again
4. Continue until the person can accurately teach back the key points

**Examples:**
- "Can you show me how you would take this medication?"
- "What signs would tell you that you need to call the doctor?"
- "Can you walk me through what you would do if [patient] fell?"

## 8. Documentation and Record-Keeping for Caregivers

### Why Documentation Matters

- Provides continuity of care between different caregivers (family, paid aides, rotating shifts)
- Creates an objective record of changes over time that helps healthcare providers make informed decisions
- Protects the caregiver by documenting actions taken and instructions received
- Helps coordinate care among multiple providers
- Supports medication management and prevents errors
- Enables accurate reporting using SBAR or other communication frameworks

### Essential Documents for Caregivers to Maintain

| Document | Contents | Location |
|----------|----------|----------|
| Medication List | All medications (prescription, OTC, supplements) with doses, timing, prescribing provider, pharmacy | Keep updated; bring to all medical appointments |
| Medical History Summary | Diagnoses, surgeries, hospitalizations, allergies, immunizations | Accessible to all caregivers; bring to appointments and ER visits |
| Healthcare Provider Directory | Names, phone numbers, fax numbers, addresses for all providers (PCP, specialists, pharmacy, home health, hospice) | By the phone or in a care binder |
| Advance Directives | Living will, healthcare proxy, POLST/DNR if applicable | Accessible to all caregivers; copy with primary care provider; copy at hospital |
| Insurance Information | Insurance cards, policy numbers, contact information, authorization requirements | In care binder; copy for ER visits |
| Emergency Contact List | Family members, neighbors, healthcare providers, poison control, hospice on-call | Posted on refrigerator; in care binder; in phone |
| Daily Care Log | Vital signs, food/fluid intake, medications given, bowel/bladder function, activity, mood, sleep, pain level, skin observations | Completed daily; reviewed at care transitions and appointments |
| Communication Log | Notes from provider calls, visit summaries, instructions received, changes to care plan | Chronological record; bring to appointments |

### Care Coordination Binder

A physical or digital care coordination binder keeps all essential information organized and accessible. Sections may include:

1. **Emergency Information** (contacts, allergies, DNR status)
2. **Medical History** (diagnoses, surgeries, hospitalizations)
3. **Medications** (current list with details)
4. **Healthcare Providers** (directory with contact information)
5. **Insurance and Financial** (cards, policy information)
6. **Legal Documents** (advance directives, power of attorney)
7. **Daily Care Log** (ongoing tracking)
8. **Communication Log** (provider interactions)
9. **Appointment Schedule** (upcoming visits, follow-ups)
10. **Notes and Instructions** (discharge instructions, therapy exercises, dietary guidelines)

### Digital Tools for Care Coordination

- **CaringBridge:** Online health journals and care coordination (caringbridge.org)
- **CareZone:** Medication management and care coordination app
- **Lotsa Helping Hands:** Calendar-based care coordination for community support (lotsahelpinghands.com)
- **MyChart and patient portals:** Access medical records, communicate with providers, view test results
- **Google Calendar or shared calendars:** Schedule appointments, medication reminders, caregiver shifts

## 9. Telehealth Communication

### Overview

Telehealth has become an increasingly important mode of healthcare delivery, especially for homebound older adults and their caregivers. Effective telehealth communication requires some adaptation from in-person visits but offers significant advantages in terms of access and convenience.

### Preparing for a Telehealth Visit

**Technical Preparation:**
- Test the video and audio connection before the appointment
- Ensure adequate lighting (face the light source, not a window)
- Choose a quiet, private location
- Charge the device fully or plug it in
- Have the provider's phone number available in case of technical difficulties
- Close unnecessary applications to improve connection quality

**Clinical Preparation (Same as In-Person):**
- Prepare questions and concerns in advance
- Have the medication list and medical history accessible
- Document any new symptoms or changes since the last visit
- If the care recipient is the patient, position them so the provider can see and hear them
- Take vital signs if possible and have the numbers ready (weight, blood pressure, temperature, blood sugar)
- Have any areas of concern visible (wounds, rashes, swelling) ready to show on camera

**During the Telehealth Visit:**
- Speak clearly and face the camera
- Describe symptoms specifically, as the provider cannot perform a physical exam
- Show any visible concerns on camera (skin changes, swelling, wounds)
- Take notes or ask the provider to send a visit summary
- Ask for clarification if the connection is poor and you did not hear clearly
- Confirm next steps, prescriptions, and follow-up before ending the call

### When Telehealth Is and Is Not Appropriate

**Good for:**
- Medication management and refills
- Follow-up on stable chronic conditions
- Reviewing test results
- Discussing care plans and goals
- Mental health support for caregivers
- Nutritional counseling
- Caregiver education and coaching

**Requires In-Person Visit:**
- New or worsening acute symptoms
- Physical examination needed (heart, lungs, abdomen, neurological)
- Procedures, vaccinations, blood draws
- Wound assessment that requires hands-on evaluation
- Cognitive testing or functional assessment
