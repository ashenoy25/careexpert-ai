## 1. Understanding Pressure Injuries (Pressure Ulcers)

### 1.1 What Is a Pressure Injury?

A pressure injury (previously called pressure ulcer, pressure sore, or bedsore) is localized damage to the skin and/or underlying tissue, usually over a bony prominence or related to a medical device. The injury occurs as a result of sustained pressure or pressure combined with shear forces. Contributing factors include moisture, poor nutrition, friction, immobility, and reduced sensation.

### 1.2 Who Is at Risk?

Individuals at highest risk include those who:
- Are bedridden or wheelchair-bound
- Cannot reposition themselves independently
- Have decreased sensation (e.g., spinal cord injury, neuropathy, diabetes)
- Have poor nutritional status or are dehydrated
- Are elderly (age 65+), especially with thin or fragile skin
- Have urinary or fecal incontinence (moisture damage)
- Have a history of previous pressure injuries
- Have conditions that impair circulation (diabetes, peripheral vascular disease)
- Are on medications that affect skin integrity (steroids, anticoagulants)
- Have cognitive impairment and cannot communicate discomfort

### 1.3 Common Anatomical Sites

Pressure injuries most commonly develop over bony prominences:
- **Supine position:** Sacrum/coccyx, heels, occiput (back of head), shoulder blades, elbows
- **Side-lying position:** Greater trochanter (hip), lateral malleolus (ankle), ear, shoulder
- **Seated position:** Ischial tuberosities (sit bones), sacrum/coccyx, shoulder blades
- **Prone position:** Forehead, chin, chest, knees, toes

Medical devices (oxygen tubing, catheters, splints, compression stockings) can also cause pressure injuries at contact points.

## 2. Pressure Injury Staging System (NPIAP Classification)

**Source:** National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP), updated staging definitions (2016, reaffirmed 2019)
**URL:** https://npiap.com/page/PressureInjuryStages

### Stage 1: Non-Blanchable Erythema of Intact Skin
- **What the caregiver sees:** An area of redness on intact skin that does not turn white (blanch) when pressed with a fingertip
- **In darker skin tones:** May appear as a persistent area of red, blue, or purple discoloration; the area may feel different from surrounding skin (warmer, cooler, firmer, softer, or painful)
- **Key sign:** The redness persists for more than 30 minutes after pressure is relieved
- **Caregiver action:** Report immediately; implement intensified prevention measures; keep pressure OFF the area entirely; do NOT massage the area

### Stage 2: Partial-Thickness Skin Loss with Exposed Dermis
- **What the caregiver sees:** A shallow open wound with a red or pink wound bed; may also appear as an intact or ruptured fluid-filled blister
- **Important:** Does NOT include skin tears, tape burns, moisture-associated skin damage (incontinence-associated dermatitis), maceration, or excoriation
- **Caregiver action:** Report to nurse/clinician immediately; do NOT attempt to treat without clinical direction; keep the area clean and protected; maintain pressure offloading

### Stage 3: Full-Thickness Skin Loss
- **What the caregiver sees:** Full-thickness tissue loss in which subcutaneous fat may be visible; bone, tendon, or muscle is NOT exposed
- **Note:** Slough (yellowish dead tissue) may be present but does not obscure the depth of tissue loss; may include undermining and tunneling
- **Caregiver action:** This requires professional wound care management; report immediately; follow wound care plan as directed by the nurse; never probe or pack a wound without training and delegation

### Stage 4: Full-Thickness Skin and Tissue Loss
- **What the caregiver sees:** Full-thickness tissue loss with exposed or directly palpable bone, tendon, fascia, muscle, cartilage, or joint capsule
- **Note:** Slough and/or eschar (black/brown dead tissue) may be present; often includes undermining and tunneling
- **Caregiver action:** Requires immediate professional wound care; this is a serious medical wound; follow the plan of care precisely; report any changes in wound appearance, size, odor, or drainage

### Unstageable: Obscured Full-Thickness Skin and Tissue Loss
- **What the caregiver sees:** Full-thickness tissue loss in which the base of the ulcer is covered by slough (yellow, tan, gray, green, or brown) and/or eschar (tan, brown, or black) in the wound bed
- **Note:** Until enough slough and/or eschar is removed to expose the base of the wound, the true depth and stage cannot be determined
- **Caregiver action:** Report to clinician; do NOT attempt to remove slough or eschar; this requires professional assessment and debridement decisions

### Deep Tissue Pressure Injury (DTPI)
- **What the caregiver sees:** Intact or non-intact skin with a localized area of persistent non-blanchable deep red, maroon, or purple discoloration, or epidermal separation revealing a dark wound bed or blood-filled blister
- **In darker skin tones:** May be difficult to detect visually; feel for changes in tissue consistency (boggy, mushy, firm) or temperature differences
- **Significance:** This indicates damage to deeper tissue from prolonged pressure and/or shear; the wound may rapidly evolve and worsen over the following days
- **Caregiver action:** Report immediately; this can deteriorate rapidly; keep all pressure off the area; monitor closely for changes

### Medical Device-Related Pressure Injury
- Pressure injury resulting from the use of devices designed for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes
- Staged using the same staging system above
- Common culprits: oxygen masks/tubing, CPAP masks, splints, casts, compression stockings, urinary catheters, feeding tubes

## 3. Risk Assessment

### 3.1 Braden Scale (Most Widely Used Risk Assessment Tool)

**Source:** Braden Scale for Predicting Pressure Sore Risk (Bergstrom, Braden, Laguzza & Holman, 1987)
**Used by:** Most hospitals, home health agencies, and long-term care facilities in the US

The Braden Scale evaluates six subscales. Total scores range from 6-23; lower scores indicate higher risk.

| Subscale | What It Measures | Score Range |
|----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Sensory perception | Ability to respond meaningfully to pressure-related discomfort | 1 (completely limited) - 4 (no impairment) |
| Moisture | Degree to which skin is exposed to moisture | 1 (constantly moist) - 4 (rarely moist) |
| Activity | Degree of physical activity | 1 (bedfast) - 4 (walks frequently) |
| Mobility | Ability to change and control body position | 1 (completely immobile) - 4 (no limitations) |
| Nutrition | Usual food intake pattern | 1 (very poor) - 4 (excellent) |
| Friction and shear | Amount of assistance required for movement and degree of sliding | 1 (problem) - 3 (no apparent problem) |

**Risk Levels:**
- 19-23: No risk
- 15-18: Mild risk
- 13-14: Moderate risk
- 10-12: High risk
- 9 or below: Very high risk

**Note for caregivers:** The nurse or supervising clinician typically completes the formal Braden Scale assessment. However, caregivers should understand the risk factors so they can observe and report changes. A caregiver who notices the patient is eating less, becoming less mobile, or experiencing increased incontinence should report these changes promptly, as they affect pressure injury risk.

### 3.2 Simplified Risk Factor Checklist for Caregivers

Caregivers should be alert if their client/patient has ANY of the following:
- [ ] Cannot change position without help (bed or chair)
- [ ] Spends most of the day in bed or a wheelchair
- [ ] Has lost weight recently or has a poor appetite
- [ ] Has urinary or fecal incontinence
- [ ] Has diabetes or poor circulation
- [ ] Has very thin, dry, or fragile skin
- [ ] Has a previous history of pressure injuries
- [ ] Has decreased sensation (cannot feel pain/pressure normally)
- [ ] Is confused or unable to communicate discomfort
- [ ] Has a recent hospitalization or surgical procedure
- [ ] Uses a medical device that contacts the skin

**Rule of thumb:** The more boxes checked, the higher the risk and the more aggressive prevention strategies must be.

## 4. Prevention Strategies: What Caregivers CAN and SHOULD Do

### 4.1 Repositioning and Mobility

**Source:** NPIAP/EPUAP/PPPIA 2019 Guideline; AHRQ Pressure Ulcer Prevention Toolkit
**Key recommendation:** Reposition at-risk individuals to reduce the duration and magnitude of pressure over vulnerable areas of the body.

#### Repositioning Schedule
- **In bed:** Reposition at least every 2 hours (or more frequently based on the individual's risk level and skin tolerance)
- **In wheelchair/chair:** Reposition at least every 1 hour; shift weight every 15-30 minutes if able
- **Use a turning schedule/clock** posted at the bedside to track repositioning times and positions
- **Night turns:** Do not skip nighttime repositioning for at-risk individuals; consider alternating pressure redistribution mattresses to potentially extend repositioning intervals per clinical guidance

#### Proper Repositioning Techniques
- Use the **30-degree lateral tilt** (tilted side-lying) rather than a full 90-degree side-lying position to avoid direct pressure on the greater trochanter
- **Never position directly on a pressure injury** or on bony prominences with non-blanchable erythema
- When repositioning, **lift, do not drag or slide** the patient; dragging causes friction and shear injury
- Use draw/turn sheets, slide boards, or mechanical lifts to reduce friction and shear during transfers and repositioning
- Elevate the **head of bed no more than 30 degrees** for prolonged periods (unless medically required) to reduce shear forces on the sacrum
- When seated, ensure the person's feet are fully supported (on the floor or footrests) to prevent sliding forward in the chair

#### Heel Protection
- Heels are the second most common site for pressure injuries
- **Float the heels** off the bed surface entirely using pillows placed lengthwise under the calves (from mid-calf to just above the Achilles tendon, leaving the heel completely suspended)
- Do NOT place pillows directly under the heel; the goal is to eliminate ALL pressure on the heel
- Commercial heel suspension devices are available and recommended for high-risk patients
- Avoid placing anything heavy on the feet (tightly tucked sheets, heavy blankets)

#### Encourage Mobility
- Assist patients with any level of movement they can tolerate (bed mobility exercises, sitting on the edge of bed, standing, walking)
- More movement = lower pressure injury risk
- Work with the physical therapist's plan if one is in place

### 4.2 Support Surfaces (Mattresses, Cushions, Overlays)

**Source:** NPIAP/EPUAP/PPPIA 2019 Guideline; WOCN Society Guidelines

#### What Caregivers Should Know
- A **pressure redistribution mattress** (specialized foam, alternating pressure, low-air-loss) should be used for individuals at moderate to high risk
- Standard hospital mattresses are NOT adequate for high-risk patients
- **Wheelchair cushions:** Pressure-redistributing cushions should be used for individuals who spend extended time in a wheelchair; the cushion should be appropriate for the individual's needs (not a donut ring -- ring cushions are harmful as they concentrate pressure)
- **Do NOT use:**
  - Donut-shaped ring cushions (they concentrate pressure around the wound edges)
  - Water-filled gloves or sheepskins as primary pressure redistribution devices
  - Devices that raise the heel but create pressure elsewhere
- Check that the mattress is performing properly: with an overlay or specialized mattress, place your hand under the patient at a bony prominence -- you should not be able to feel the underlying surface (this is called "bottoming out" and means the surface is inadequate)
- Report any equipment malfunction (deflated mattress, alarm sounding) immediately

### 4.3 Skin Inspection and Care

**Source:** NPIAP/EPUAP/PPPIA 2019 Guideline; NICE CG179

#### Daily Skin Assessment
- **Inspect the skin at least once daily** (during bathing or repositioning) focusing on all bony prominences
- Pay special attention to: sacrum/coccyx, heels, hips (greater trochanters), ischial tuberosities, elbows, shoulder blades, ears, and the back of the head
- **In darker skin tones**, visual assessment alone is insufficient; also assess for:
  - Temperature changes (localized warmth or coolness compared to surrounding tissue)
  - Tissue consistency changes (boggy, firm, or mushy areas)
  - Pain or tenderness at the site
  - Color changes (persistent red, blue, purple, or shiny/taut appearance)
- Check under and around all medical devices (O2 tubing, catheters, splints, stockings)
- **Document and report ANY changes** in skin condition to the supervising nurse

#### Skin Care Practices
- Keep skin clean and dry; cleanse promptly after any incontinence episode
- Use **pH-balanced, no-rinse cleansers** when possible rather than soap and water (soap can dry and irritate fragile skin)
- **Moisturize dry skin** daily, especially on extremities (use fragrance-free, hypoallergenic moisturizers)
- **Do NOT massage or vigorously rub** reddened areas or skin over bony prominences (this can cause additional tissue damage)
- **Apply barrier creams/ointments** (dimethicone-based or petrolatum-based) to skin exposed to moisture from incontinence
- Avoid excessive skin moisture from perspiration, wound drainage, or incontinence
- Avoid excessive skin dryness and cracking
- Keep bed linens smooth and wrinkle-free (wrinkled linens create uneven pressure points)

### 4.4 Moisture Management and Incontinence Care

**Source:** NPIAP/EPUAP/PPPIA 2019; WOCN Incontinence-Associated Dermatitis Guidelines

#### Key Practices
- Incontinence is a major risk factor for both pressure injuries and incontinence-associated dermatitis (IAD)
- **Cleanse skin gently and promptly** after every incontinence episode
- Use incontinence cleansers (no-rinse, pH balanced) rather than soap and water
- Apply a **moisture barrier product** (dimethicone-based cream, zinc oxide ointment, or petrolatum-based barrier) to protect skin exposed to urine or stool
- Use **absorbent incontinence products** (briefs, pads) that wick moisture away from the skin; change promptly when soiled
- Avoid plastic-backed products directly against the skin; they trap moisture and heat
- Monitor for signs of **incontinence-associated dermatitis (IAD)**: redness, inflammation, erosion, or maceration (white, soggy-looking skin) in the perineal area, buttocks, inner thighs, or skin folds
- Report persistent redness in perineal/buttock area, as IAD can be confused with a Stage 1 or Stage 2 pressure injury; clinical differentiation is important for treatment
- **Establish a toileting schedule** when possible to reduce incontinence episodes
- Ensure adequate fluid intake (hydration helps maintain skin integrity and does NOT worsen incontinence)

### 4.5 Nutrition and Hydration

**Source:** NPIAP/EPUAP/PPPIA 2019 Guideline; ASPEN Clinical Guidelines

#### What Caregivers Should Know and Do
- **Adequate nutrition is essential** for both prevention and healing of pressure injuries
- Report any of the following to the nurse/dietitian:
  - Patient eating less than half of meals consistently
  - Unintentional weight loss
  - Difficulty chewing or swallowing
  - Refusing meals or losing interest in food
  - Signs of dehydration (dark urine, dry mouth, dizziness, confusion)
- Encourage adequate fluid intake (typically 6-8 cups/day unless medically restricted)
- Offer protein-rich foods at every meal and snack (eggs, yogurt, cheese, beans, chicken, fish, peanut butter, protein supplements)
- Offer fruits and vegetables for vitamins and minerals essential to skin health (especially vitamin C and zinc)
- Provide small, frequent meals if appetite is poor
- Honor food preferences to maximize intake
- A **registered dietitian** should evaluate any patient with a pressure injury or at high nutritional risk

#### Caregiver Scope Note
- Caregivers CAN encourage and assist with eating, monitor intake, report concerns
- Caregivers should NOT prescribe specific supplements or diets; this is a clinical decision
- If nutritional supplements (e.g., protein drinks, vitamin supplements) are prescribed, caregivers can help ensure they are taken as directed

### 4.6 Friction and Shear Reduction

#### What Caregivers Can Do
- Use **lift/draw sheets** for repositioning; never drag the patient across bed linens
- Apply **skin protectants or transparent film dressings** over vulnerable areas prone to friction (elbows, heels) if directed by the nurse
- Keep **heels elevated** off the bed surface
- Use **elbow and heel protectors** (foam or sheepskin sleeves) as an adjunct (NOT as a replacement for repositioning)
- Avoid raising the head of the bed above 30 degrees for extended periods unless medically necessary (higher elevation increases shear at the sacrum)
- Use **transfer aids** (slide boards, mechanical lifts, transfer belts) during transfers
- Keep skin moisturized to reduce friction
- Ensure properly fitted clothing and footwear (no bunching or wrinkles)

## 5. Skin Tear Prevention

**Source:** International Skin Tear Advisory Panel (ISTAP) Best Practice Recommendations; LeBlanc et al., Advances in Skin & Wound Care (2018)
**URL:** https://www.skintears.org/

### 5.1 What Is a Skin Tear?

A skin tear is a traumatic wound caused by mechanical forces (friction, shear, or blunt force) that results in separation of the skin layers. Skin tears are extremely common in the elderly due to age-related changes that make the skin thin, fragile, and less elastic.

### 5.2 Risk Factors for Skin Tears
- Advanced age (75+)
- History of previous skin tears
- Dry, fragile, or papery skin
- Use of corticosteroids or blood thinners
- Impaired mobility (dependence on caregivers for transfers)
- Cognitive impairment (bumping into objects, agitation)
- Poor nutrition and hydration
- Edema of extremities

### 5.3 Prevention Strategies for Caregivers

#### Environmental Modifications
- Ensure adequate lighting to prevent bumps and falls
- Pad sharp corners on furniture, bed rails, and wheelchair parts
- Remove or pad any rough surfaces the patient may contact
- Keep pathways clear of obstacles
- Ensure wheelchair footrests and armrests have padding

#### Skin Care for Tear Prevention
- **Moisturize the skin twice daily** (especially forearms and shins, which are most vulnerable)
- Use fragrance-free, hypoallergenic moisturizers
- Pat skin dry gently; never rub vigorously
- Avoid adhesive tape directly on fragile skin; use paper tape, silicone tape, or tubular net bandages
- When removing adhesive dressings or tape, peel slowly in the direction of hair growth while supporting the skin
- Use skin protectant sleeves (long sleeves, shin guards) for individuals at high risk

#### Safe Handling Techniques
- **Never grip or grasp** the patient's skin or extremities during transfers or repositioning
- Use the **palms of the hands** (flat, open hand) when assisting; avoid grasping with fingertips
- Use **draw sheets and mechanical lifts** to prevent friction during repositioning
- Ensure jewelry (rings, watches) does not scrape patient's skin during care
- Avoid pulling on clothing when dressing/undressing; use gentle techniques
- Dress the patient in **long sleeves and pants** made of soft fabric to protect arms and legs
- Keep fingernails (caregiver's AND patient's) trimmed and smooth

#### First Aid for Skin Tears (What Caregivers CAN Do)
1. Control bleeding with gentle pressure using a clean, non-stick pad
2. Gently cleanse the wound with saline or clean water (do NOT use hydrogen peroxide or alcohol on skin tears)
3. If there is a skin flap, gently lay it back into place (do not remove it); use a moistened cotton-tipped applicator if needed
4. Cover with a non-adherent dressing (silicone-based dressing is ideal for fragile skin)
5. Report the skin tear to the nurse/supervisor; document its location, size, and appearance
6. Do NOT use adhesive tape directly on fragile surrounding skin to secure dressings

## 6. When to Escalate: Red Flags Requiring Immediate Clinical Attention

### ALWAYS report to the supervising nurse or clinician immediately if you observe:

#### Skin Changes
- [ ] New area of redness that does not blanch (turn white) when pressed
- [ ] Any open wound, blister, or skin breakdown
- [ ] Skin discoloration (purple, maroon, or dark areas) that appears suddenly
- [ ] Area of skin that feels unusually warm, cool, hard, boggy, or mushy
- [ ] Skin breakdown under or around a medical device

#### Wound Changes (If a Wound Already Exists)
- [ ] Wound is getting larger, deeper, or changing shape
- [ ] New or increased drainage from the wound
- [ ] Drainage that is green, yellow, or foul-smelling (signs of possible infection)
- [ ] Increased pain at the wound site
- [ ] Black or dark tissue (eschar) appearing in the wound
- [ ] Red streaks extending from the wound
- [ ] Wound edges becoming ragged, rolled, or separated
- [ ] Exposed bone, tendon, or muscle visible in the wound

#### Systemic Signs
- [ ] Fever or chills (may indicate wound infection or sepsis)
- [ ] Increased confusion or change in mental status
- [ ] Patient reporting new or worsening pain at a bony prominence
- [ ] Significant decrease in food or fluid intake
- [ ] New or worsening incontinence

#### General Rule
**When in doubt, report it.** It is always better to report a concern that turns out to be benign than to miss an early warning sign. Pressure injuries can worsen rapidly if not identified and addressed early.

## 7. Scope of Practice: What Caregivers CAN Do vs. What Requires a Medical Professional

### What HHAs, CNAs, and Family Caregivers CAN Do

| Activity | Details |
|----------|---------|
| Skin inspection | Perform daily head-to-toe skin checks; know what normal looks like for the individual |
| Repositioning | Follow the turning/repositioning schedule; use proper techniques |
| Basic skin care | Cleanse, moisturize, apply barrier creams as directed |
| Incontinence care | Prompt cleansing, barrier application, product changes |
| Nutrition support | Encourage eating and drinking, offer protein-rich snacks, monitor intake |
| Mobility assistance | Help with ambulation, transfers, range-of-motion exercises as directed |
| Environmental safety | Ensure equipment is working, bed linens smooth, environment free of hazards |
| Heel offloading | Position pillows to float heels; apply heel protectors |
| Documentation | Record skin findings, repositioning times, food/fluid intake, any concerns |
| Reporting | Communicate any skin changes or concerns to the supervising clinician promptly |
| Skin tear first aid | Control bleeding, gentle cleansing, reapproximate skin flap, apply non-adherent dressing, report |
| Support surface checks | Ensure mattress/cushion is inflated and functioning; check for "bottoming out" |

### What REQUIRES a Nurse, Wound Care Specialist, or Physician

| Activity | Why It Requires a Professional |
|----------|-------------------------------|
| Formal risk assessment (Braden Scale) | Requires clinical judgment and interpretation |
| Wound staging and classification | Requires trained clinical assessment |
| Wound measurement and documentation | Standardized clinical measurement techniques |
| Debridement (removing dead tissue) | Requires clinical training; can cause harm if done incorrectly |
| Wound dressing selection and orders | Requires knowledge of wound types, products, and healing |
| Negative pressure wound therapy (wound vac) | Specialized medical device management |
| Prescribing nutritional supplements | Requires dietary/medical assessment |
| Ordering support surfaces/devices | Requires clinical assessment of risk level and needs |
| Assessing for wound infection | Requires clinical evaluation and possible cultures |
| Prescribing antibiotics or topical agents | Medical/nursing scope; requires prescription authority |
| Determining repositioning schedule modifications | Clinical decision based on overall assessment |
| Differentiating IAD from pressure injury | Requires clinical expertise for differential diagnosis |

## 8. Practical Quick-Reference: Daily Prevention Checklist for Caregivers

Use this checklist every shift/visit:

### Morning / Start of Shift
- [ ] Perform full skin inspection during AM care/bathing (all bony prominences, under devices)
- [ ] Report any new skin changes to supervising nurse
- [ ] Check that the mattress/cushion is functioning (not bottomed out, properly inflated)
- [ ] Ensure heels are floating off the bed surface
- [ ] Apply moisturizer to dry skin areas
- [ ] Apply barrier cream to perineal area if patient is incontinent

### Throughout the Day
- [ ] Reposition every 2 hours in bed (use 30-degree lateral tilt, document times)
- [ ] Reposition every 1 hour in chair; encourage weight shifts every 15-30 minutes
- [ ] Manage incontinence promptly (cleanse, barrier, change products)
- [ ] Encourage adequate food and fluid intake at each meal
- [ ] Offer protein-rich snacks between meals
- [ ] Encourage any tolerated mobility and movement
- [ ] Keep bed linens smooth and wrinkle-free
- [ ] Minimize head-of-bed elevation when possible

### End of Shift
- [ ] Perform final skin check during PM care
- [ ] Document repositioning schedule compliance
- [ ] Document food/fluid intake
- [ ] Report any concerns to incoming caregiver and supervising nurse
- [ ] Ensure nighttime repositioning plan is in place

## 9. Key Evidence-Based Principles Summary

### NPIAP/EPUAP/PPPIA 2019 International Guideline - Core Prevention Recommendations

1. **Risk assessment:** Conduct a structured risk assessment as soon as possible (within 8 hours of admission to care) and reassess regularly and whenever the individual's condition changes
2. **Skin assessment:** Perform a comprehensive skin assessment at entry to care, regularly thereafter, and with any change in condition; include assessment of skin temperature and moisture
3. **Repositioning:** Reposition all at-risk individuals unless contraindicated; consider the individual's condition, the support surface in use, and the individual's comfort
4. **Nutrition:** Screen nutritional status for all individuals at risk; refer to a dietitian for assessment when nutritional risk is identified
5. **Microclimate management:** Keep the skin appropriately moisturized; protect skin from excessive moisture exposure
6. **Support surfaces:** Select an appropriate support surface based on risk level, number and severity of existing pressure injuries, and comfort
7. **Medical device-related pressure injury prevention:** Inspect skin under and around medical devices at least twice daily
8. **Education:** Provide education to the individual, family members, and caregivers about pressure injury prevention

### NICE CG179 Key Points (2014, last checked 2019)
- Adults at risk should have skin assessment at first episode of care and at least weekly thereafter
- Use a validated scale (Braden or Waterlow) for risk assessment
- High-specification reactive foam mattresses should be used for at-risk adults (not standard hospital mattresses)
- Repositioning should use the 30-degree tilt technique
- Skin should NOT be massaged over pressure areas

## 10. Glossary of Terms for Caregivers

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Blanching** | When skin turns white (blanches) under fingertip pressure, then returns to its normal color -- this is NORMAL. Non-blanching redness is a concern. |
| **Bony prominence** | An area where bone is close to the skin surface (sacrum, heels, hips, elbows, etc.) |
| **Bottoming out** | When a support surface is compressed to the point where it no longer redistributes pressure adequately; tested by sliding a hand between the patient and the support surface |
| **Debridement** | Medical removal of dead or damaged tissue from a wound (clinical procedure) |
| **Erythema** | Redness of the skin |
| **Eschar** | Black or brown dead tissue covering a wound |
| **Excoriation** | Superficial skin loss from scratching or chemical irritation |
| **IAD** | Incontinence-Associated Dermatitis -- skin inflammation from exposure to urine/stool |
| **Maceration** | Softening and breakdown of skin from prolonged moisture exposure (skin looks white, wrinkled, soggy) |
| **NPIAP** | National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (formerly NPUAP - National Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel) |
| **Offloading** | Removing pressure from a specific body area (e.g., floating heels off the bed) |
| **Pressure redistribution** | Distributing pressure over a larger surface area (e.g., using a specialized mattress or cushion) |
| **Shear** | A force caused by layers of tissue sliding against each other; occurs when the body slides down in bed while the skin remains stationary |
| **Skin flap** | A piece of skin that has partially separated (as in a skin tear) but is still attached |
| **Slough** | Yellow, tan, or gray dead tissue in a wound |
| **Support surface** | A specialized mattress, mattress overlay, or cushion designed to redistribute pressure |
| **Undermining** | Tissue destruction extending under intact skin edges; detectable by probing (clinical assessment) |
