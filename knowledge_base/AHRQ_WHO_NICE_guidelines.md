## 1A. STEADI-Related Fall Prevention Resources

**Organization:** Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), U.S. Department of Health and Human Services
**Resource Name:** Falls Prevention Resources / STEADI-Aligned Tools
**URL:** https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/settings/long-term-care/resource/injuries/fallspx/index.html
**Additional URLs:**
- AHRQ Falls Management Program: https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/settings/long-term-care/resource/injuries/fallspx/index.html
- CDC STEADI (primary source): https://www.cdc.gov/steadi/index.html
- AHRQ Fall Prevention Toolkit: https://www.ahrq.gov/professionals/systems/hospital/fallpxtoolkit/index.html

### Overview

AHRQ provides fall prevention resources that complement the CDC's STEADI initiative. While STEADI is primarily a CDC program, AHRQ has developed hospital-based and long-term care fall prevention toolkits that are relevant to home care transitions and caregiver education. AHRQ's resources focus on system-level approaches to fall prevention, patient engagement, and the transition from institutional to home-based care.

### Key Topics Covered

1. **Falls Prevention in Hospital Settings (Toolkit)**
   - Universal fall precautions for all patients
   - Post-fall assessment protocols (the "huddle" approach)
   - Risk assessment using validated tools
   - Individualized fall prevention plans
   - Patient and family education about fall risk

2. **Falls Prevention in Long-Term Care**
   - Environmental assessment checklists
   - Medication review protocols for fall-risk medications
   - Exercise and mobility programs
   - Multifactorial risk reduction strategies
   - Staff training on fall prevention

3. **Transition-Relevant Tools**
   - Discharge planning that includes fall risk communication
   - Home safety assessment before discharge
   - Caregiver education on fall risk factors
   - Follow-up protocols after fall-related hospitalizations

### Key Recommendations Relevant to Non-Medical Caregivers

- **Home environment modification**: Remove tripping hazards, improve lighting, install grab bars in bathrooms, secure loose rugs, ensure clear pathways
- **Medication awareness**: Be aware that certain medications (sedatives, blood pressure medications, antidepressants) increase fall risk; report dizziness or unsteadiness to supervising clinician
- **Mobility monitoring**: Observe and report changes in gait, balance, or steadiness; ensure proper use of assistive devices (walkers, canes)
- **Footwear**: Ensure the care recipient wears well-fitting, non-skid footwear; discourage walking in socks or loose slippers
- **Post-fall response**: Know what to do after a fall -- assess for injury before moving the person, document the circumstances, report to the supervising nurse or clinician
- **Exercise support**: Encourage and assist with prescribed exercise and balance programs
- **Vision and environmental awareness**: Ensure adequate lighting, especially at night and on stairs; remove obstacles from walking paths

## 1B. Patient Safety Resources Relevant to Home Care

**Organization:** AHRQ
**Resource Name:** Patient Safety in Home Health Care / Patient Safety Network (PSNet)
**URLs:**
- AHRQ Patient Safety Network: https://psnet.ahrq.gov/
- Home Health Care Safety: https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/settings/ambulatory/index.html
- Making Healthcare Safer reports: https://www.ahrq.gov/research/findings/making-healthcare-safer/index.html
- TeamSTEPPS: https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps/index.html

### Overview

AHRQ's Patient Safety Network (PSNet) is a comprehensive repository of patient safety research, evidence, and resources. While originally focused on hospital and ambulatory care settings, many of the safety frameworks, communication tools, and error prevention strategies are directly applicable to home care. AHRQ's Making Healthcare Safer reports identify evidence-based practices that reduce harm.

### Key Topics Covered

1. **Communication Failures and Handoffs**
   - SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) communication framework
   - Structured handoff protocols between care settings
   - Family/caregiver inclusion in care communication

2. **Medication Safety in Home Settings**
   - Medication reconciliation at care transitions
   - High-alert medications in home care
   - Polypharmacy risks in elderly patients
   - Caregiver role in medication safety

3. **Infection Prevention in Home Care**
   - Hand hygiene as foundational practice
   - Catheter-associated infection prevention
   - Wound care safety
   - Environmental cleaning protocols for home settings

4. **Diagnostic Safety**
   - Recognizing symptom changes that require escalation
   - Caregiver observation and reporting
   - Warning signs framework

5. **TeamSTEPPS for Home Care**
   - Team communication strategies adapted for home care teams
   - Shared mental models between caregivers and clinicians
   - Mutual support and situation monitoring
   - Assertive communication about safety concerns (CUS framework: "I am Concerned, I am Uncomfortable, this is a Safety issue")

### Key Recommendations Relevant to Non-Medical Caregivers

- **Use structured communication**: When reporting to a nurse or clinician, use SBAR format -- state the Situation, provide Background, share your Assessment, and make a Recommendation or request
- **Document observations**: Keep a log of daily observations including vital signs trends, behavioral changes, skin changes, appetite, and sleep patterns
- **Medication reconciliation**: Maintain an up-to-date medication list; bring it to every medical appointment; flag discrepancies
- **Speak up about safety concerns**: Use the CUS framework -- say "I am Concerned," "I am Uncomfortable," or "This is a Safety issue" when something seems wrong
- **Infection prevention**: Practice consistent hand hygiene, follow clean technique for catheter care, monitor for signs of infection
- **Recognize "red flags"**: Learn the warning signs that require immediate escalation (sudden confusion, chest pain, severe shortness of breath, signs of stroke)

## 1C. Guide to Patient and Family Engagement

**Organization:** AHRQ
**Resource Name:** Guide to Patient and Family Engagement in Hospital Quality and Safety
**URL:** https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/patients-families/engagingfamilies/index.html
**Additional URL:** https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/patients-families/index.html

### Overview

AHRQ's Guide to Patient and Family Engagement provides evidence-based strategies and tools for healthcare organizations and caregivers to actively engage patients and their families in care. While originally designed for the hospital setting, the principles and many of the tools are directly transferable to home care. The guide emphasizes that engaged patients and families experience better outcomes and fewer adverse events.

### Key Topics Covered

1. **Strategy 1: Working with Patients and Families as Advisors**
   - Establishing patient and family advisory councils
   - Including caregivers in quality improvement

2. **Strategy 2: Communicating to Improve Quality**
   - The IDEAL Discharge Planning process
     - **I**nclude the patient and family as full partners in discharge planning
     - **D**iscuss key areas with the patient and family (diagnosis, medications, warning signs, follow-up)
     - **E**ducate the patient and family in plain language
     - **A**ssess how well the patient and family understand
     - **L**isten to and honor the patient and family's goals and preferences

3. **Strategy 3: Nurse Bedside Shift Report**
   - Including patients/families in shift reports
   - Transparency in care communication

4. **Strategy 4: SHARE Approach to Shared Decision-Making**
   - **S**eek your patient's participation
   - **H**elp your patient explore and compare treatment options
   - **A**ssess your patient's values and preferences
   - **R**each a decision with your patient
   - **E**valuate your patient's decision

5. **Care After Hospital Discharge**
   - Teach-back method for verifying caregiver understanding
   - Written discharge instructions in plain language
   - Follow-up appointment scheduling
   - Medication changes communication
   - Red flag/warning sign education

### Key Recommendations Relevant to Non-Medical Caregivers

- **Be an active participant**: Ask questions during medical visits, request information in plain language, take notes
- **Use teach-back**: After receiving instructions, repeat them back in your own words to confirm understanding ("Let me make sure I understand...")
- **Know the care plan**: Understand the diagnosis, medications, activity restrictions, diet requirements, and follow-up appointments
- **Learn warning signs**: Know the specific signs and symptoms that should trigger a call to the clinician or 911
- **Keep organized records**: Maintain a care notebook or binder with medical information, medication lists, provider contacts, and insurance information
- **Advocate for the care recipient**: Speak up when something does not seem right; you know the person best and your observations matter
- **Ask about medication changes**: At every care transition, ask "Has anything changed with the medications?"
- **Prepare for appointments**: Write down questions in advance, bring the medication list, and bring a summary of recent changes

## 2A. iSupport for Dementia

**Organization:** World Health Organization (WHO)
**Resource Name:** iSupport for Dementia -- A WHO Online Programme for Carers of People Living with Dementia
**URL:** https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241515863
**Additional URL:** https://www.iSupport-dementia.org
**Publication Year:** 2019

### Overview

iSupport is a WHO-developed, evidence-based online program designed to provide information, skills training, and support to caregivers (informal carers) of people living with dementia. It is based on problem-solving and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) principles. The program was developed to address the global need for accessible, scalable dementia caregiver support, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where formal support services may be limited.

iSupport can be used as:
- A self-help online program (accessible via web browser)
- A facilitated program delivered by trained non-specialist workers
- A printed manual for settings without internet access

### Program Structure

iSupport is organized into **5 modules** with **23 lessons** total:

#### Module 1: Introduction to Dementia
- What is dementia?
- Types of dementia (Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, Lewy body, frontotemporal)
- How dementia progresses over time
- Understanding the symptoms and their impact on daily life
- Available treatments and services

#### Module 2: Being a Carer
- Understanding the caregiving role
- Impact of caregiving on the carer's own life
- Problem-solving skills for everyday caregiving challenges
- Seeking help and building a support network
- Planning for the future (legal, financial, care planning)

#### Module 3: Caring for Me (Caregiver Self-Care)
- Recognizing and managing stress
- Coping with negative thoughts and emotions
- Relaxation and mindfulness techniques
- Maintaining physical health
- Managing feelings of guilt, anger, and grief
- Finding time for activities you enjoy
- When and how to seek professional help for yourself

#### Module 4: Providing Everyday Care
- Communication strategies for different stages of dementia
- Assisting with activities of daily living (bathing, dressing, eating, toileting)
- Managing nutrition and hydration
- Promoting physical activity and engagement
- Supporting continence
- Sleep management
- Pain recognition and management in people who cannot communicate effectively

#### Module 5: Dealing with Behaviour Changes
- Understanding that behavior is a form of communication
- Identifying triggers for behavioral changes
- Managing agitation and aggression
- Coping with wandering
- Addressing hallucinations and delusions
- Handling repetitive questions and behaviors
- Managing resistance to care
- Sundowning strategies
- Strategies for dealing with apathy and withdrawal

### Key Topics Covered

- Dementia education and awareness
- Practical caregiving skills for ADLs
- Behavioral and psychological symptom management
- Caregiver mental health and self-care
- Problem-solving and coping strategies (CBT-based)
- Communication techniques
- Future planning and accessing services

### Key Recommendations Relevant to Non-Medical Caregivers

- **Understand the disease**: Learning about dementia helps caregivers anticipate changes, reduce frustration, and respond more effectively
- **Use problem-solving approaches**: When facing a caregiving challenge, define the problem clearly, brainstorm solutions, evaluate options, try the best one, and review the results
- **Practice self-care as a priority**: Caregiver burnout is a significant risk; schedule regular breaks, maintain social connections, and seek support
- **Communicate effectively**: Use simple, clear language; give one instruction at a time; maintain eye contact; use a calm, reassuring tone; avoid arguing or correcting
- **Respond to behaviors, not just manage them**: Look for underlying causes of behavioral changes (pain, hunger, overstimulation, boredom, fear) rather than just trying to stop the behavior
- **Adapt the environment**: Simplify the living space, maintain consistent routines, use visual cues and labels, ensure safety
- **Plan ahead**: Discuss legal matters, future care preferences, and financial planning while the person can still participate in decisions
- **Seek support**: Use available community resources, support groups, respite care, and professional guidance

## 2B. Home-Based Care for Older Adults

**Organization:** World Health Organization (WHO)
**Resource Name:** Home-Based Long-Term Care (Technical Report Series 898) and Integrated Continuum of Long-Term Care
**URLs:**
- WHO Technical Report on Home-Based Long-Term Care: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/home-based-long-term-care
- WHO Ageing and Health: https://www.who.int/health-topics/ageing
- WHO Global Report on Ageing and Health (2015): https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241565042

### Overview

WHO has produced multiple frameworks and technical reports addressing home-based care for older adults. The overarching theme is that home-based care should be the preferred setting for long-term care when possible, as it promotes dignity, autonomy, and quality of life. WHO's approach emphasizes that home-based care requires adequate support systems for both the older person and their caregivers.

### Key Topics Covered

1. **Framework for Home-Based Long-Term Care**
   - Definition: Long-term care encompasses activities undertaken by others to ensure that a person who is not fully capable of self-care can maintain the highest possible quality of life, according to their own preferences, with the greatest degree of independence, autonomy, participation, personal fulfillment, and human dignity
   - Home-based care as the preferred setting within a continuum of care
   - Integration of health and social services at home

2. **Functional Ability and Intrinsic Capacity**
   - Focus on maintaining and optimizing functional ability rather than treating diseases alone
   - Intrinsic capacity: the composite of all physical and mental capacities of an individual
   - Functional ability: the combination of intrinsic capacity, the environment, and their interaction
   - Environments (including home environments) can be modified to compensate for declining intrinsic capacity

3. **Caregiver Support as Essential Infrastructure**
   - Recognition that informal caregivers (family, friends) provide the majority of long-term care globally
   - Caregivers need training, information, respite, and financial support
   - Caregiver well-being is directly linked to quality of care provided
   - Policy recommendations for social protection of caregivers

4. **Person-Centered and Rights-Based Approach**
   - Older persons have the right to make decisions about their care
   - Care should be adapted to individual needs, preferences, and values
   - Prevention of elder abuse and neglect
   - Ensuring dignity in all care interactions

5. **Community-Based Support Systems**
   - Day care and respite services
   - Community health worker programs
   - Volunteer support networks
   - Technology-assisted care and telehealth
   - Integration with primary health care

### Key Recommendations Relevant to Non-Medical Caregivers

- **Focus on what the person can do**: Support independence by encouraging the person to do as much as they can for themselves; assist only where needed
- **Adapt the home environment**: Modify the living space to support functional ability -- improve lighting, remove hazards, install assistive devices, simplify layouts
- **Monitor for changes in functional ability**: Track changes in the person's ability to perform daily activities; report declining function early
- **Maintain social connections**: Isolation is a major risk for older adults at home; facilitate social engagement, visits, phone/video calls, and community participation
- **Provide holistic care**: Address physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs -- not just medical tasks
- **Prevent abuse and neglect**: Be aware of signs of elder abuse (physical, emotional, financial, neglect); understand reporting obligations
- **Access community resources**: Connect with local support services including adult day programs, meal delivery, transportation assistance, and support groups
- **Take care of yourself**: Sustainable caregiving requires attending to your own health and well-being

## 2C. ICOPE -- Integrated Care for Older People

**Organization:** World Health Organization (WHO)
**Resource Name:** Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE): Guidelines on Community-Level Interventions to Manage Declines in Intrinsic Capacity
**URLs:**
- ICOPE Guidelines: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-FWC-ALC-18.1
- ICOPE Implementation Framework: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240010529
- ICOPE Handbook: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240017900
- ICOPE Screening Tool (app): https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/WHO-FWC-ALC-19.1
**Publication Year:** 2017 (guidelines), 2019 (implementation framework), 2021 (handbook)

### Overview

ICOPE is WHO's flagship framework for managing age-related declines in physical and mental capacities. It represents a paradigm shift from disease-centered to person-centered care for older adults. ICOPE focuses on maintaining and improving intrinsic capacity and functional ability through community-level interventions, with strong relevance to home-based caregiving.

The ICOPE approach uses a stepped care model:
1. **Step 1**: Screen for declines in intrinsic capacity (community/home level)
2. **Step 2**: Person-centered assessment in primary care
3. **Step 3**: Develop a personalized care plan
4. **Step 4**: Ensure the care pathway links to geriatric specialist care when needed
5. **Step 5**: Engage communities and support caregivers

### Key Domains Assessed and Addressed by ICOPE

#### Domain 1: Cognitive Decline
- Screening: Memory and orientation questions
- Interventions: Cognitive stimulation, physical exercise, management of cardiovascular risk factors, social engagement
- Caregiver relevance: Recognizing early signs, providing cognitive activities, adapting communication

#### Domain 2: Limited Mobility
- Screening: Chair rise test, mobility assessment
- Interventions: Multicomponent exercise (balance, strength, aerobic, flexibility), environmental modifications, assistive devices
- Caregiver relevance: Assisting with exercise programs, fall prevention, safe transfers, mobility support

#### Domain 3: Malnutrition
- Screening: Weight loss assessment, appetite evaluation
- Interventions: Nutritional supplementation, dietary counseling, oral health care, addressing underlying causes
- Caregiver relevance: Meal preparation, monitoring intake, observing signs of malnutrition, ensuring adequate hydration

#### Domain 4: Visual Impairment
- Screening: Near-vision testing
- Interventions: Referral for eye examination, corrective lenses, environmental adaptation (lighting, contrast)
- Caregiver relevance: Ensuring appropriate lighting, keeping environment consistent, assisting with vision aids

#### Domain 5: Hearing Loss
- Screening: Whisper test or audiometry
- Interventions: Hearing aids, communication strategies, environmental modifications
- Caregiver relevance: Clear communication techniques, reducing background noise, ensuring hearing aid maintenance

#### Domain 6: Depressive Symptoms
- Screening: Questions about mood and interest in activities
- Interventions: Psychological interventions (CBT, problem-solving therapy), physical activity, social participation, referral for pharmacological treatment if needed
- Caregiver relevance: Monitoring mood changes, encouraging social engagement, recognizing when to escalate

### Additional ICOPE Focus Areas

- **Urinary incontinence**: Assessment and management including pelvic floor exercises, bladder training, environmental modifications
- **Risk of falls**: Multifactorial assessment and intervention (exercise, home modification, medication review, vision correction)
- **Caregiver support**: Assessment of caregiver needs, training, respite, psychological support
- **Polypharmacy**: Medication review, deprescribing where appropriate
- **Advance care planning**: Establishing goals of care, documenting preferences

### Key Recommendations Relevant to Non-Medical Caregivers

- **Screen and observe**: Use simple screening approaches to detect changes in cognition, mobility, nutrition, vision, hearing, and mood; report findings to the care team
- **Promote physical activity**: Encourage and assist with regular exercise, including balance and strength training -- this is the single most impactful intervention across multiple domains
- **Ensure adequate nutrition**: Monitor weight, appetite, and food intake; prepare nutrient-dense meals; ensure adequate hydration; assist with oral health
- **Support sensory function**: Ensure glasses and hearing aids are clean, functional, and used; optimize the home environment for sensory limitations
- **Monitor mood and social engagement**: Watch for signs of depression or withdrawal; encourage social activities, meaningful engagement, and community participation
- **Prevent falls**: Implement a comprehensive approach including exercise, environmental modification, footwear, medication awareness, and vision/hearing optimization
- **Manage incontinence with dignity**: Establish toileting routines, modify the environment for easy bathroom access, use appropriate continence products, and maintain skin care
- **Support cognitive health**: Provide cognitive stimulation through conversation, puzzles, reading, music, and reminiscence; maintain structured routines
- **Coordinate care**: Communicate observations across the care team; maintain updated records of functional changes

# PART 3: NICE (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence)

## 3A. NG21 -- Home Care: Delivering Personal Care and Practical Support to Older People Living in Their Own Homes

**Organization:** National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), United Kingdom
**Resource Name:** Home care: delivering personal care and practical support to older people living in their own homes (NG21)
**URL:** https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng21
**Publication Date:** September 2015 (last updated: checked regularly against current evidence)
**Related Quality Standard:** QS123 -- Home care for older people (2016)

### Overview

NICE Guideline NG21 is the most comprehensive evidence-based guideline specifically addressing home care for older adults. It covers the full spectrum of home care delivery, from commissioning and organizing services to the direct provision of personal care and practical support. The guideline is designed for home care providers, commissioners, care workers, older people, and their families/carers.

NG21 applies to adults (aged 18+) who need home care, but is particularly focused on older people. It addresses both regulated personal care and practical support services.

### Scope and Definitions

- **Personal care**: Help with eating, washing, dressing, toileting, and managing medications (regulated activity)
- **Practical support**: Help with household tasks such as shopping, laundry, meal preparation, and managing finances (may or may not be regulated)
- **Home care**: Care and support delivered in a person's own home

### Key Recommendation Areas

#### 1. Ensuring Care is Person-Centered

- **Involve the person in all decisions** about their care from the outset
- Carry out a **comprehensive assessment** of needs, preferences, goals, and outcomes
- Develop a **written care and support plan** that the person has contributed to and agreed upon
- Review care plans **regularly** and whenever circumstances change
- Ensure the person's **cultural, religious, and personal preferences** are respected in all aspects of care
- Support the person's **independence and autonomy** -- help them to do things for themselves rather than doing everything for them
- Ensure the person knows **how to raise concerns or complaints** and feels confident doing so

#### 2. Planning, Organizing, and Managing Home Care

- **Allocate enough time** for each home care visit to carry out all planned tasks without rushing
- **Include travel time** separately from care time in scheduling
- Ensure **continuity of care** by assigning a consistent team of care workers to each person wherever possible
- Provide **introductions** when a new care worker begins working with someone
- Use **call monitoring and electronic scheduling systems** transparently and with the person's knowledge
- **Avoid 15-minute visits** for personal care unless the person's needs genuinely require only brief contact
- **Sequential visits** (one person after another on a route) should not compromise the quality or timing of care

#### 3. Delivering Personal Care and Practical Support

- **Personal care** should be delivered in a way that maintains the person's **dignity and privacy**
- Help with **bathing, dressing, toileting, eating, mobility, and grooming** should follow the care plan and respect the person's preferences
- **Meal preparation** should account for dietary needs, preferences, and any medical conditions
- **Medication support** should follow the care plan and local protocols; care workers should know what they are and are not authorized to do with medications
- **Practical support** (cleaning, shopping, laundry) should be tailored to the person's preferences and agreed schedule
- Support with **social activities and maintaining relationships** should be included in care planning

#### 4. Supporting Care Workers (Workforce)

- Provide **comprehensive induction and ongoing training** for all care workers
- Training should cover: person-centered care, safeguarding, medication management, dementia awareness, end-of-life care, nutrition and hydration, falls prevention, skin integrity, infection prevention, communication skills, and cultural awareness
- Ensure care workers have **supervision and support**, including regular one-to-one meetings
- Support care workers' **well-being and professional development**
- Pay care workers fairly, including for **travel time** between visits
- Ensure care workers have adequate **time and resources** to deliver quality care

#### 5. Safeguarding

- All care workers should be trained in **recognizing signs of abuse and neglect** (physical, emotional, sexual, financial, institutional, discriminatory, and self-neglect)
- Clear **reporting procedures** for safeguarding concerns
- Understand **local safeguarding policies** and the role of the local safeguarding adults board
- **Mental capacity** should be assessed when there are concerns about a person's ability to make decisions
- Apply the **Mental Capacity Act** principles: assume capacity unless established otherwise; support the person to make their own decisions; unwise decisions do not mean lack of capacity

#### 6. Managing Transitions

- When care needs change, ensure **smooth transitions** between services (e.g., from hospital to home care, from home care to residential care)
- Communicate effectively with all parties during transitions
- Review and update the care plan after any transition

### Key Recommendations Relevant to Non-Medical Caregivers

- **Follow the care plan**: Deliver care according to the agreed care and support plan; report deviations or concerns to your supervisor
- **Maintain dignity and respect**: Always knock before entering, explain what you are going to do, provide privacy during personal care, address the person by their preferred name
- **Support independence**: Encourage the person to do as much as they can for themselves; adapt your approach based on their abilities, which may fluctuate day to day
- **Observe and report**: Watch for changes in physical health, mood, skin integrity, appetite, mobility, and cognitive function; report changes promptly
- **Communicate effectively**: Listen actively, speak clearly, allow time for responses, use the person's preferred communication method
- **Be consistent**: Follow routines and preferences documented in the care plan; consistency reduces anxiety and supports well-being
- **Know your scope**: Understand what you are and are not authorized to do, especially regarding medication support; when in doubt, ask your supervisor
- **Safeguarding awareness**: Know the signs of abuse and neglect; follow reporting procedures without delay
- **Record-keeping**: Document the care provided, observations, and any incidents accurately and promptly

## 3B. Other NICE Guidelines Relevant to Home Caregiving

### 3B.1 Dementia: Assessment, Management, and Support (NG97)

**URL:** https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng97
**Publication Date:** June 2018

#### Overview
NG97 provides comprehensive guidance on dementia care including diagnosis, pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions, and support for carers. It has a strong emphasis on person-centered care and carer support.

#### Key Topics Relevant to Caregivers
- Non-pharmacological interventions for behavioral symptoms (group cognitive stimulation therapy, tailored activities, music therapy, aromatherapy, massage)
- Carer education and support programs (structured, multicomponent interventions)
- Assessment and management of care needs across dementia stages
- Advance care planning
- Managing specific behavioral symptoms (agitation, aggression, psychosis, sleep disturbance)
- Pain assessment using validated observational tools for people who cannot self-report

#### Key Recommendations for Non-Medical Caregivers
- Offer carers an assessment of their own needs, and provide information about available support
- Carers should be offered psychoeducation and skills training (managing symptoms, communication, self-care, accessing services)
- Structured exercise programs should be offered to people with dementia
- Do not use antipsychotic medications as first-line treatment for non-cognitive symptoms (try non-pharmacological approaches first)
- Use standardized pain assessment tools (e.g., Abbey Pain Scale, PAINAD) for people with dementia who cannot report pain verbally

#### Taxonomy Mapping
Domains 2 (Dementia), 12 (Caregiver Self-Care), 1 (ADLs), 13 (Communication), 10 (Chronic Pain)

### 3B.2 Falls in Older People: Assessing Risk and Prevention (CG161)

**URL:** https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg161
**Publication Date:** June 2013 (last reviewed regularly)

#### Overview
CG161 provides evidence-based recommendations for identifying older people at risk of falling and interventions to prevent falls. It covers community-dwelling, hospital, and care home settings.

#### Key Topics Relevant to Caregivers
- Multifactorial risk assessment components (fall history, gait/balance, medications, vision, cognitive impairment, continence, home hazards)
- Multifactorial interventions: strength and balance training, home hazard assessment and modification, vision assessment, medication review
- Exercise programs: at least 2 sessions per week for balance and strength; Tai Chi recommended
- Home safety assessment and modification by trained professionals
- Medication review focusing on psychotropic drugs

#### Key Recommendations for Non-Medical Caregivers
- Ask about fall history -- a previous fall is the strongest predictor of future falls
- Encourage and assist with prescribed exercise and balance programs
- Ensure the home is assessed for hazards and modifications are implemented
- Ensure vision is checked annually and corrective lenses are current
- Be aware that certain medications (sedatives, antihypertensives, opioids, anticonvulsants) increase fall risk
- Ensure proper footwear (well-fitting, non-skid soles)

#### Taxonomy Mapping
Domains 3 (Safety & Fall Prevention - Primary), 4 (Medication Management), 1 (ADLs - Mobility)

### 3B.3 Older People with Social Care Needs and Multiple Long-Term Conditions (NG22)

**URL:** https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng22
**Publication Date:** November 2015

#### Overview
NG22 addresses the needs of older people (aged 65+) with multiple long-term conditions who use or need social care. It emphasizes integrated, person-centered approaches and the importance of care coordination.

#### Key Topics Relevant to Caregivers
- Integrated care planning across health and social care
- Identifying and assessing people who may benefit from an integrated approach
- Named care coordinator role
- Anticipatory care and crisis prevention
- Medicines optimization for people with multimorbidity
- Supporting self-management
- Carer assessment and support

#### Key Recommendations for Non-Medical Caregivers
- Ensure the person has a single, named care coordinator
- Contribute to and understand the integrated care plan
- Support medication management and report concerns about polypharmacy
- Watch for early signs of deterioration that could be prevented
- Support the person in self-management where possible
- Ensure carers receive their own assessment of needs and access to support

#### Taxonomy Mapping
Domains 4 (Medication Management), 6 (When to Escalate), 13 (Communication), 12 (Caregiver Self-Care)

**URL:** https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ph16
**Publication Date:** October 2008

#### Overview
PH16 focuses on promoting mental well-being in people aged 65 and older through occupational therapy and physical activity interventions.

#### Key Topics Relevant to Caregivers
- Tailored physical activity programs for older adults
- Occupational therapy to maintain independence in daily activities
- Group-based and individual activities to prevent isolation and depression
- Adapting activities to functional ability

#### Key Recommendations for Non-Medical Caregivers
- Encourage regular physical activity tailored to the person's abilities
- Support participation in group activities and social engagement
- Assist with meaningful occupations and hobbies
- Watch for signs of depression, isolation, or reduced engagement

# PART 5: Summary and Integration Notes

## Key Themes Across All Three Organizations

### 1. Person-Centered Care Is Foundational
All three organizations emphasize that care should be organized around the person's needs, preferences, and goals rather than around disease categories or service structures. This applies directly to non-medical caregivers who should consistently ask "What matters to you?" rather than just "What is the matter with you?"

### 2. Caregiver Support Is Not Optional
WHO (iSupport, ICOPE Step 5), AHRQ (Family Engagement Guide), and NICE (NG21 workforce recommendations, NG97 carer interventions) all recognize that supporting caregivers is essential to sustainable, high-quality care. Caregiver assessment, training, respite, and psychological support are consistently recommended.

### 3. Fall Prevention Requires a Multicomponent Approach
AHRQ (STEADI), WHO (ICOPE mobility domain), and NICE (CG161) all converge on the same evidence base: fall prevention requires exercise/balance training, medication review, home environment modification, vision correction, and proper footwear. No single intervention is sufficient.

### 4. Communication and Escalation Skills Are Critical
AHRQ (SBAR, CUS, teach-back), WHO (ICOPE screening and reporting), and NICE (NG21 reporting, safeguarding) all emphasize that caregivers need structured approaches to communicate observations and concerns to the clinical team.

### 5. Scope of Practice Awareness
All three organizations implicitly or explicitly acknowledge that non-medical caregivers operate within defined boundaries. NICE NG21 is most explicit about what care workers can and cannot do. AHRQ's escalation frameworks help caregivers know when to hand off to clinical professionals.

### 6. Environmental Modification Is a High-Impact Intervention
Across all guidelines, adapting the home environment (lighting, clutter removal, grab bars, assistive devices, safety modifications) is recognized as one of the most practical and effective things caregivers can do to prevent injury and support independence.

## Gaps and Complementary Coverage

- **Condition-specific care** (Domains 7-10: diabetes, heart failure, stroke, pain): These are less covered by AHRQ, WHO, and NICE at the caregiver-specific level. The knowledge base relies on disease-specific organizations (ADA, AHA) for these domains.
- **Infection control**: AHRQ/PSNet and NICE NG21 address this, but the primary source remains CDC guidelines (already documented separately).
- **Legal/ethical frameworks**: NICE NG21 provides the most detailed guidance on safeguarding, mental capacity, and client rights. AHRQ and WHO address these at a higher level.

## Integration Recommendations for CareExpert AI

1. **Use AHRQ STEADI resources** as the primary framework for fall prevention screening questions and home safety checklists in the AI system
2. **Use WHO iSupport** as the evidence-based model for dementia caregiver support content -- its modular structure (5 modules, 23 lessons) aligns well with a structured AI knowledge base
3. **Use NICE NG21** as the primary reference for care delivery standards, particularly around dignity, scope of practice, safeguarding, and person-centered care planning
4. **Use WHO ICOPE** as the screening and assessment framework for detecting changes across the six key domains (cognition, mobility, nutrition, vision, hearing, mood)
5. **Use AHRQ Family Engagement tools** (IDEAL, SHARE, teach-back) for caregiver communication training and discharge/transition support
6. **Use AHRQ PSNet/TeamSTEPPS** for structured communication frameworks (SBAR, CUS) that caregivers can use when reporting to clinical teams
