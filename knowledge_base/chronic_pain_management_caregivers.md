## 1. Understanding Chronic Pain in Older Adults

### 1.1 What Is Chronic Pain?

Chronic pain is pain that persists or recurs for more than 3 months. It may or may not have an identifiable cause. In older adults, chronic pain is extremely common and often undertreated. It differs from acute pain (which is short-lived and typically signals tissue damage) in that chronic pain may persist long after the original injury has healed or may have no clear structural cause.

### 1.2 Prevalence and Impact
- Chronic pain affects an estimated 25-50% of community-dwelling older adults and up to 80% of nursing home residents
- Common sources include osteoarthritis, low back pain, neuropathic pain (diabetic neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia), musculoskeletal conditions, cancer, and post-surgical pain
- Chronic pain significantly impacts quality of life, contributing to depression, anxiety, social isolation, sleep disturbances, decreased mobility, functional decline, and falls
- Pain is frequently underreported and undertreated in older adults, especially those with cognitive impairment

### 1.3 Why Non-Pharmacological Approaches Matter
- Medications carry significant risks in older adults (side effects, drug interactions, falls, cognitive impairment, kidney/liver effects)
- The CDC 2022 Clinical Practice Guideline for Prescribing Opioids recommends non-pharmacological therapies as preferred first-line treatments for many chronic pain conditions
- Non-pharmacological strategies can reduce pain intensity, improve function, and enhance quality of life either alone or in combination with medications
- Many non-pharmacological approaches can be safely supported by caregivers in the home setting
- These approaches empower the person to participate actively in their own pain management

### 1.4 Pain Assessment Basics for Caregivers

#### Why Assessment Matters
Pain cannot be effectively managed if it is not recognized and communicated. Caregivers play a critical role in observing, recognizing, and reporting pain.

#### Pain Assessment for Verbal/Communicative Patients
- Ask about pain regularly using simple, direct language: "Are you hurting anywhere?" "Tell me about your pain today"
- Use a **Numeric Rating Scale (NRS):** "On a scale of 0 to 10, where 0 is no pain and 10 is the worst pain imaginable, what is your pain right now?"
- Ask about pain **location, quality** (sharp, dull, burning, aching, throbbing), **timing** (constant vs. intermittent, better/worse at certain times), and **what makes it better or worse**
- Ask about the **impact on function:** "Does the pain keep you from doing anything?" "How does it affect your sleep?"

#### Pain Assessment for Non-Verbal or Cognitively Impaired Patients
Use behavioral observation. Watch for the following signs:

| Category | Signs to Observe |
|----------|-----------------|
| **Facial expressions** | Frowning, grimacing, clenched jaw, rapid blinking, tearing, frightened look |
| **Verbalizations/vocalizations** | Moaning, groaning, sighing, calling out, aggressive speech, noisy breathing |
| **Body movements** | Rigid, tense posture, guarding, fidgeting, rocking, restricted movement, pacing |
| **Changes in activity** | Refusing food, increased wandering, change in sleep pattern, sudden withdrawal from activities |
| **Changes in interpersonal interactions** | Aggression, resistance to care, withdrawal, combativeness (especially during repositioning, bathing, or dressing) |
| **Mental status changes** | Increased confusion, irritability, crying |

**Validated tools for non-verbal patients include:**
- PAINAD Scale (Pain Assessment in Advanced Dementia) - 5 behavioral categories scored 0-2 each
- Abbey Pain Scale
- FLACC Scale (more common in pediatrics but adapted for non-verbal adults)

**Important:** Behavioral changes during caregiving activities (such as resistance to bathing, guarding during transfers, or agitation during repositioning) may indicate pain. These should be reported to the supervising clinician.

## 2. Non-Pharmacological Pain Management Techniques

### 2.1 Positioning and Body Mechanics

**Source:** APTA; AGS; Arthritis Foundation
**Caregiver role:** CAN implement with training

#### Principles
- Proper body alignment reduces stress on joints, muscles, and tissues
- Misalignment or prolonged static positioning can worsen pain
- Good positioning in bed, chair, and during activities can significantly reduce pain

#### Positioning in Bed
- Use pillows to support the natural curves of the spine and to keep joints in neutral alignment
- **Side-lying:** Place a pillow between the knees to align the hips and reduce lower back stress; support the upper arm with a pillow to prevent shoulder strain
- **Supine (on back):** Place a pillow under the knees to reduce lower back strain; support the head and neck with a pillow that maintains neutral spine alignment
- **Semi-reclined:** When the head of bed must be elevated, place pillows under the arms for support and a pillow under the knees to reduce strain
- Avoid positions that place stress on affected joints (e.g., do not position directly on a painful hip)
- Change position regularly (every 1-2 hours) to prevent stiffness and increased pain from immobility

#### Positioning in a Chair/Wheelchair
- Feet should be flat on the floor or supported on footrests (dangling feet increases lower back and hip pain)
- Hips and knees should be at approximately 90-degree angles
- Lumbar (lower back) support with a rolled towel or small pillow
- Armrests should support the forearms without elevating the shoulders
- Avoid prolonged sitting in one position; encourage standing or shifting weight every 30-60 minutes
- Use cushions to redistribute pressure and improve comfort

#### Transfer and Mobility Techniques
- Use proper body mechanics during transfers to minimize pain for the patient
- Move slowly and gently; avoid jerky or sudden movements
- Give advance warning before moving the patient: "I'm going to help you turn now"
- Support painful joints during movement (e.g., support a painful shoulder during transfers)
- Use assistive devices (gait belt, transfer board, mechanical lift) to reduce strain on both patient and caregiver
- Allow the patient to move at their own pace when possible

### 2.2 Heat Therapy (Thermotherapy)

**Source:** NCCIH; Arthritis Foundation; AGS
**Caregiver role:** CAN apply with proper precautions

#### How It Works
Heat increases blood flow, relaxes muscles, reduces stiffness, and may decrease pain signals. It is most effective for chronic muscle and joint pain, stiffness, and muscle spasm.

#### Methods
- **Warm moist towel/compress:** Soak a towel in warm (not hot) water, wring out, and apply to the painful area
- **Heating pad (electric):** Use on low-to-medium setting with a cloth barrier between the pad and skin
- **Warm water soak:** Soak hands or feet in warm water for 15-20 minutes
- **Microwavable heat packs:** Follow manufacturer instructions; test temperature before applying
- **Warm blankets:** Can provide general comfort and mild therapeutic benefit

#### Application Guidelines
- Apply heat for **15-20 minutes** at a time (maximum 30 minutes)
- Allow at least 1 hour between heat applications
- **ALWAYS place a barrier** (towel, pillowcase) between the heat source and the skin
- **Test the temperature** on the inside of YOUR forearm before applying to the patient
- Check the skin under the heat source every 5 minutes for signs of excessive redness, burns, or discomfort

#### Safety Precautions and Contraindications
**Do NOT apply heat to:**
- Open wounds or broken skin
- Areas with decreased sensation (neuropathy, numbness) -- the patient may not feel burns
- Acutely inflamed, swollen, or hot joints (heat can worsen acute inflammation)
- Areas of impaired circulation (severe peripheral vascular disease)
- Over areas with deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or suspected DVT
- Directly over a skin cancer or tumor

**Additional precautions:**
- Never use heat with patients who cannot communicate discomfort or remove the heat source themselves, unless the caregiver can closely monitor
- Never allow the patient to lie on top of a heating pad (body weight increases burn risk)
- Never use heat while the patient is sleeping (risk of prolonged unmonitored exposure)
- If skin becomes excessively red or the patient reports increased pain, remove the heat source immediately and report

### 2.3 Cold Therapy (Cryotherapy)

**Source:** NCCIH; Arthritis Foundation; APTA
**Caregiver role:** CAN apply with proper precautions

#### How It Works
Cold reduces blood flow to an area, decreasing swelling and inflammation. It numbs the area and can reduce pain signals. It is most effective for acute pain flares, swollen/inflamed joints, and after physical activity that causes pain.

#### Methods
- **Cold pack / ice pack:** Wrap a commercial cold pack or bag of ice in a towel; never apply directly to skin
- **Frozen vegetable bag:** A bag of frozen peas conforms well to body contours
- **Cold towel:** Soak a towel in cold water or wrap ice cubes in a damp towel
- **Cold water soak:** For hands or feet

#### Application Guidelines
- Apply cold for **10-15 minutes** at a time (maximum 20 minutes)
- Allow at least 1 hour between cold applications
- **ALWAYS place a barrier** (towel, cloth) between the cold source and the skin
- Watch for excessive pallor, numbness, or skin color changes

#### Safety Precautions and Contraindications
**Do NOT apply cold to:**
- Areas with decreased sensation or neuropathy
- Areas of impaired circulation (Raynaud's phenomenon, severe peripheral vascular disease)
- Open wounds (unless specifically directed by clinician)
- Over areas of cold hypersensitivity or cold urticaria

**Do NOT use cold therapy in individuals with:**
- Cryoglobulinemia
- Cold intolerance conditions
- Severe Raynaud's disease

### 2.4 Contrast Therapy (Alternating Heat and Cold)

- Some patients benefit from alternating warm and cold applications
- Typical protocol: 3-4 minutes warm, followed by 1 minute cold, repeated 3-4 times, ending with warm
- Particularly useful for hand and foot stiffness/pain (e.g., arthritis)
- Follow all the safety precautions for both heat and cold therapy
- Should be initiated only with clinical guidance

### 2.5 Gentle Exercise and Movement

**Source:** AGS; APTA; Arthritis Foundation; CDC Physical Activity Guidelines
**Caregiver role:** CAN assist and encourage within the plan of care

#### Why Movement Helps
- Regular, gentle movement is one of the most effective non-pharmacological pain management strategies
- Exercise releases endorphins (natural pain-relieving chemicals)
- Reduces stiffness, improves joint mobility, strengthens muscles that support painful joints
- Improves mood, sleep, and overall function
- Inactivity and immobility typically worsen chronic pain over time

#### Types of Movement Caregivers Can Support
- **Range of motion (ROM) exercises:** Gentle movements that take joints through their full range; can be active (patient does it), active-assistive (patient does it with help), or passive (caregiver moves the joint). Follow the physical therapist's specific instructions for each joint.
- **Walking:** Even short distances (to the bathroom, around the room, to the mailbox) can be therapeutic; increase gradually
- **Chair exercises:** Seated exercises for patients who cannot stand safely; ankle pumps, knee extensions, arm raises, shoulder rolls
- **Stretching:** Gentle stretching held for 15-30 seconds (no bouncing); focus on areas of stiffness
- **Water-based exercise:** If accessible, warm water provides buoyancy that reduces joint stress (pool exercise, warm tub soaking with gentle movements)
- **Tai Chi / adapted Tai Chi:** Evidence-based for chronic pain in older adults; slow, flowing movements that improve balance, flexibility, and pain

#### Activity Pacing
This is a critical concept for chronic pain management:
- **What it is:** Breaking activities into smaller segments with planned rest breaks, rather than pushing through until pain forces a stop (the "boom-bust" cycle)
- **Why it matters:** Patients with chronic pain often have "good days" where they overdo it, leading to "bad days" of severe pain and inability to function. Pacing prevents this cycle.
- **How caregivers can help:**
  - Encourage the patient to plan activities and spread them throughout the day
  - Build in scheduled rest breaks BEFORE pain becomes severe
  - Use a timer if helpful (e.g., 15 minutes of activity, 10 minutes of rest)
  - Alternate demanding activities with lighter ones
  - Encourage the patient to do a consistent amount each day rather than "saving up" for big efforts
  - If the patient says "I feel good today, I'll do everything," gently encourage moderation
  - Track activity levels and pain patterns to identify optimal pacing

#### Key Safety Principles
- Always follow the physical therapist's or physician's exercise recommendations
- Never force a joint past the point of resistance or significant pain
- "Some discomfort" during gentle exercise may be normal; "sharp, increasing, or severe pain" is a signal to stop
- Warm up before and cool down after exercise
- Stop exercise and report to the clinician if the patient experiences dizziness, shortness of breath, chest pain, or significantly increased pain

### 2.6 Relaxation Techniques

**Source:** NCCIH; ACPA; AGS; NICE NG193
**Caregiver role:** CAN guide and facilitate

#### Deep Breathing Exercises
- One of the simplest and most accessible pain management tools
- **Technique (Diaphragmatic Breathing):**
  1. Have the patient sit or lie comfortably
  2. Place one hand on the chest and one on the abdomen
  3. Breathe in slowly through the nose for 4 counts, feeling the abdomen rise (the chest should remain relatively still)
  4. Hold for 1-2 seconds
  5. Exhale slowly through the mouth for 6 counts (longer exhale than inhale activates the parasympathetic nervous system)
  6. Repeat for 5-10 minutes
- Can be used during painful procedures, during pain flares, or as a daily practice
- Caregivers can guide the patient through this technique and breathe along with them

#### Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR)
- Involves systematically tensing and then releasing different muscle groups
- **Technique:**
  1. Start with the feet: tense the muscles for 5 seconds, then release and relax for 15-30 seconds
  2. Move upward through calves, thighs, abdomen, hands, arms, shoulders, neck, face
  3. Focus on the contrast between tension and relaxation
  4. Full session takes approximately 15-20 minutes
- **Modification for patients with pain:** Skip or modify the tensing phase for painful body areas; instead, simply focus on relaxing those areas
- Caregivers can read PMR scripts to guide the patient
- Audio recordings are available from many pain management organizations

#### Guided Imagery / Visualization
- The patient is guided to imagine a peaceful, pleasant scene using all senses (sight, sound, smell, touch, taste)
- **Example:** "Imagine you are sitting on a warm beach. Feel the warm sand beneath you, hear the gentle waves, feel a light breeze on your skin, smell the salt air..."
- Sessions typically last 10-20 minutes
- Caregivers can read guided imagery scripts or play audio recordings
- Research supports its effectiveness for reducing chronic pain intensity and improving quality of life

#### Music Therapy / Listening
- Listening to preferred music has been shown to reduce pain perception, anxiety, and depressive symptoms
- Encourage the patient to choose music they find calming or enjoyable
- Can be used during painful procedures (dressing changes, repositioning, exercises), during rest periods, or as a daily practice
- No special equipment needed beyond a device to play music
- Even humming or singing along can be therapeutic

#### Mindfulness and Meditation
- Mindfulness involves paying attention to the present moment without judgment
- **Simple mindfulness exercise:** Focus attention on the breath, bodily sensations, or sounds in the environment; when the mind wanders, gently bring attention back without self-criticism
- Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) has strong evidence for chronic pain management
- Many free audio guides and apps are available (Insight Timer, UCLA Mindful, etc.)
- Caregivers can create a quiet, calm environment and prompt the practice

### 2.7 Distraction Techniques

**Source:** ACPA; AGS; Pain Management Nursing Literature
**Caregiver role:** CAN implement and facilitate

#### Principles
- Distraction works by redirecting attention away from pain
- It does not eliminate pain but reduces the perception of pain intensity
- Most effective when the distraction is genuinely engaging and enjoyable

#### Distraction Methods Caregivers Can Facilitate
- **Conversation:** Engaging, interesting conversation about topics the patient cares about
- **Television, movies, audiobooks:** Absorbing content that captures attention
- **Puzzles, games, crafts:** Crossword puzzles, card games, knitting, coloring, jigsaw puzzles
- **Reading aloud** to the patient
- **Reminiscence:** Looking through photo albums, telling life stories, sharing memories
- **Spiritual practices:** Prayer, scripture reading, meditation -- based on the patient's preferences and beliefs
- **Pet therapy / animal interaction:** If pets are present and the patient enjoys animals
- **Humor/laughter:** Watching comedy shows, sharing jokes; laughter releases endorphins
- **Social connection:** Facilitating phone calls, video calls with family and friends; social isolation worsens pain perception

#### During Painful Procedures
- Plan distraction activities BEFORE beginning a painful procedure (dressing change, exercise, repositioning)
- Have the distraction ready and initiated before starting the procedure
- Conversation, deep breathing, music, or counting can all help during brief painful activities

### 2.8 Massage and Touch

**Source:** NCCIH; NICE NG193; Systematic Reviews in Pain Management
**Caregiver role:** Limited; requires clinical guidance

#### What Caregivers CAN Do
- **Gentle hand/foot massage:** Light, circular pressure on the hands, feet, shoulders, or back can be soothing and reduce pain perception
- **Therapeutic touch/holding hands:** Simple human contact can be comforting and reduce anxiety-related pain amplification
- **Application of lotion during massage:** Combines skin care with gentle tactile stimulation

#### Massage Guidelines for Caregivers
- Use gentle, light-to-moderate pressure; never apply deep pressure
- Ask the patient about pressure preference and adjust accordingly
- Warm lotion or oil in your hands before applying
- Avoid massaging directly over: inflamed or swollen joints, open wounds, bruises, fractures, areas of deep vein thrombosis, skin infections, or tumor sites
- If the patient takes blood thinners, use very light pressure (increased bruising risk)
- A 5-10 minute hand or foot massage can be both comforting and pain-reducing
- Always ask permission before touching; respect the patient's preferences

#### What Requires a Professional
- Deep tissue massage
- Myofascial release
- Trigger point therapy
- Massage therapy as a formal treatment modality (requires licensed massage therapist)
- Any massage in the presence of complex medical conditions (cancer, blood clots, osteoporosis)

### 2.9 Transcutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation (TENS)

**Source:** NCCIH; NICE NG193; APTA
**Caregiver role:** CAN apply if trained and delegated by clinician

- TENS involves the use of a small, battery-operated device that delivers mild electrical impulses through electrodes placed on the skin near the painful area
- It works by stimulating nerve fibers that can block pain signals and by promoting endorphin release
- Evidence supports its use for certain chronic pain conditions (particularly musculoskeletal pain)
- **Caregiver role:** If a TENS unit has been prescribed and the clinician has trained the caregiver on its use (electrode placement, settings, duration), the caregiver may assist with application
- **Do NOT:** Adjust settings beyond what was prescribed; apply electrodes to different locations than directed; use near water; use on patients with pacemakers or implantable defibrillators without cardiology clearance; apply over the carotid arteries (front of neck), over the eyes, or over open wounds

### 2.10 Environmental and Comfort Modifications

**Source:** AGS; ACPA; NICE NG193
**Caregiver role:** CAN implement

#### Creating a Pain-Supportive Environment
- **Temperature control:** Ensure the room is comfortably warm (cold environments increase muscle tension and pain)
- **Comfortable bedding:** Supportive mattress, adequate pillows, warm blankets, smooth sheets
- **Lighting:** Reduce harsh lighting; use soft, adjustable lighting, especially during pain flares
- **Noise reduction:** Minimize unnecessary noise; offer earplugs or white noise if the environment is noisy
- **Accessibility:** Place frequently needed items within easy reach to minimize painful reaching/stretching
- **Assistive devices:** Ensure proper use of canes, walkers, reachers, jar openers, button hooks, and other adaptive equipment that reduces pain during daily activities
- **Ergonomic modifications:** Raised toilet seats, grab bars, shower chairs, elevated chairs that are easier to get in/out of

#### Sleep Support
- Chronic pain and poor sleep form a vicious cycle (pain disrupts sleep; poor sleep worsens pain)
- **Caregiver strategies:**
  - Maintain a consistent sleep schedule (same bedtime and wake time daily)
  - Ensure comfortable positioning with adequate pillow support
  - Create a dark, quiet, cool sleeping environment
  - Limit caffeine intake after noon
  - Encourage gentle activity during the day (inactivity worsens insomnia)
  - Use relaxation techniques (deep breathing, guided imagery) before bedtime
  - Apply heat therapy to painful areas before sleep
  - Report persistent sleep problems to the clinician (may indicate need for medication adjustment or sleep evaluation)

## 3. Activity Modification and Energy Conservation

### 3.1 Principles of Activity Modification

The goal is not to avoid activity (which worsens pain over time) but to modify HOW activities are performed to reduce pain.

#### Joint Protection Principles (Especially for Arthritis Pain)
- Use the largest joint available for a task (e.g., carry a bag over the shoulder/forearm rather than gripping with the fingers)
- Distribute loads over multiple joints (e.g., use both hands to lift a pot rather than one hand)
- Avoid prolonged gripping, pinching, or sustained positions
- Use assistive devices: jar openers, built-up utensil handles, button hooks, long-handled reachers, electric can openers
- Avoid tight grips; use ergonomic tools with wide, padded handles
- Respect pain signals; pain that lasts more than 2 hours after an activity suggests the activity was too much

#### Energy Conservation Techniques
- **Plan ahead:** Identify the most important tasks for the day and prioritize them
- **Organize work spaces:** Keep frequently used items at waist height and within easy reach
- **Sit when possible:** Sit for tasks like meal preparation (use a stool at the counter), grooming, and folding laundry
- **Eliminate unnecessary tasks:** Simplify routines where possible
- **Use labor-saving devices:** Electric appliances, wheeled carts, long-handled tools
- **Rest before fatigue:** Take breaks before becoming exhausted (proactive rest, not reactive rest)
- **Alternate heavy and light tasks:** Intersperse demanding activities with rest or lighter activities throughout the day

### 3.2 Helping with Daily Activities

Caregivers can assist by:
- Setting up the environment for safer, less painful task performance
- Pre-positioning items the patient needs
- Offering assistance with the most painful components of tasks while encouraging independence in less painful components
- Using proper body mechanics during assisted activities to protect both patient and caregiver
- Recognizing when the patient needs a break and offering one proactively
- Tracking which activities trigger the most pain and communicating this to the care team

## 4. When to Escalate Pain Concerns

### 4.1 Red Flags Requiring Immediate Reporting

**ALWAYS report to the supervising nurse, clinician, or call 911 as appropriate if you observe:**

#### Emergency (Call 911)
- [ ] New chest pain, especially with shortness of breath, sweating, or radiating to arm/jaw
- [ ] Sudden, severe headache unlike any the patient has had before ("worst headache of my life")
- [ ] Signs of stroke: sudden facial drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty
- [ ] Severe abdominal pain with rigid abdomen, fever, or vomiting blood
- [ ] Loss of consciousness associated with pain

#### Urgent - Report to Nurse/Clinician Same Day
- [ ] New or significantly worsening pain that does not respond to usual management strategies
- [ ] Pain that changes in character (e.g., a dull ache becomes sharp, burning, or shooting)
- [ ] Pain in a new location, especially if it suggests a fall injury or fracture
- [ ] Pain accompanied by fever (may indicate infection)
- [ ] Pain associated with new swelling, redness, or warmth in a joint (may indicate gout, septic joint, or DVT)
- [ ] Pain with new numbness, tingling, or weakness in an extremity
- [ ] Pain that prevents the patient from performing basic functions (eating, sleeping, toileting)
- [ ] Severe pain with leg swelling (may indicate deep vein thrombosis)
- [ ] Signs of medication side effects: excessive drowsiness, confusion, constipation, nausea, itching, difficulty breathing (especially with opioid medications)
- [ ] Patient expressing desire to harm themselves due to pain (suicidal ideation -- this is a medical emergency)

#### Report at Next Clinical Visit or Per Protocol
- [ ] Gradual increase in pain over days/weeks despite current management
- [ ] Current pain management strategies becoming less effective
- [ ] Patient requesting more pain medication than prescribed
- [ ] Functional decline (patient can do less than before) related to pain
- [ ] Mood changes (increased depression, anxiety, irritability) related to pain
- [ ] Sleep disturbance worsening due to pain
- [ ] Patient avoiding activities or social interactions due to pain
- [ ] Pain interfering with therapy/exercise program compliance

### 4.2 Documentation and Communication

When reporting pain concerns, include:
1. **Location:** Where is the pain? (use specific body area)
2. **Intensity:** Pain rating on 0-10 scale; or behavioral observations for non-verbal patients
3. **Quality:** What does it feel like? (sharp, dull, burning, aching, throbbing, shooting)
4. **Timing:** When did it start or change? Is it constant or intermittent? When is it worst?
5. **Aggravating/alleviating factors:** What makes it worse? What makes it better?
6. **Impact on function:** What can the patient no longer do because of the pain?
7. **Current management:** What has been tried? How effective was it?
8. **Patient's own report:** What does the patient say about the pain in their own words?

## 5. Scope of Practice: What Caregivers CAN Do vs. What Requires a Medical Professional

### What HHAs, CNAs, and Family Caregivers CAN Do

| Activity | Details |
|----------|---------|
| Pain observation and reporting | Observe verbal and behavioral pain cues; document and report using structured communication |
| Positioning and repositioning | Proper positioning in bed and chair; frequent position changes; pillow support |
| Heat application | Warm compresses, heating pads with safety precautions; within guidelines described above |
| Cold application | Cold packs with proper barriers and timing; within guidelines described above |
| Gentle exercise support | Assist with prescribed ROM exercises, walking, and seated exercises |
| Activity pacing coaching | Help the patient plan activities, take breaks, and maintain consistent activity levels |
| Relaxation techniques | Guide deep breathing, PMR, guided imagery; play relaxation recordings |
| Distraction activities | Provide and engage in conversation, games, music, reading, social activities |
| Gentle massage | Light hand/foot/back massage with approval; use of lotion |
| Environmental comfort | Adjust temperature, lighting, noise; ensure comfortable bedding and seating |
| Sleep support | Establish routines, use relaxation techniques, comfortable positioning |
| Assistive device use | Ensure proper use of canes, walkers, reachers, adaptive equipment |
| TENS application | Only if specifically trained and delegated by the clinician |
| Monitoring and documentation | Track pain levels, effectiveness of strategies, functional changes |
| Emotional support | Active listening, validation, companionship, encouragement |

### What REQUIRES a Nurse, Physician, Physical Therapist, or Other Professional

| Activity | Why It Requires a Professional |
|----------|-------------------------------|
| Comprehensive pain assessment | Requires clinical history-taking, physical exam, differential diagnosis |
| Prescribing or adjusting medications | Requires medical license and pharmacological expertise |
| Administering injections for pain | Requires nursing or medical license |
| Joint injections (corticosteroid, etc.) | Medical procedure |
| Prescribing or modifying an exercise program | Requires PT/OT assessment and clinical judgment |
| Prescribing TENS settings and electrode placement | Requires clinical assessment and orders |
| Deep tissue massage / manual therapy | Requires licensed practitioner |
| Acupuncture | Requires licensed acupuncturist or trained practitioner |
| Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for pain | Requires licensed psychologist or trained therapist |
| Diagnosing the cause of pain | Requires medical evaluation, imaging, lab work |
| Determining when pain indicates a new or worsening condition | Clinical judgment and diagnostic workup |
| Making decisions about pain management plan changes | Requires interdisciplinary team assessment |
| Ordering imaging or diagnostic tests | Requires medical provider order |

## 6. Practical Quick-Reference: Daily Pain Management Checklist for Caregivers

### Morning / Start of Shift
- [ ] Ask about pain (or observe behavioral cues): location, intensity (0-10), quality
- [ ] Ask about sleep quality and any nighttime pain
- [ ] Assist with morning positioning for comfort
- [ ] Apply heat or cold therapy if part of the daily routine
- [ ] Ensure pain medications are taken as prescribed (if in scope)
- [ ] Plan the day's activities with pacing in mind (most demanding tasks when energy is highest)

### Throughout the Day
- [ ] Monitor pain levels before, during, and after activities
- [ ] Implement pacing: activity followed by scheduled rest breaks
- [ ] Assist with prescribed exercises / ROM activities
- [ ] Offer heat/cold therapy as needed for pain flares
- [ ] Use distraction and relaxation techniques during pain flares or painful procedures
- [ ] Encourage movement and mobility within tolerance
- [ ] Facilitate social engagement and pleasant activities
- [ ] Provide gentle massage if appropriate and desired
- [ ] Reposition for comfort at least every 1-2 hours

### Evening / End of Shift
- [ ] Assess pain status; document the day's pain levels and what strategies were used
- [ ] Prepare for sleep: relaxation routine, comfortable positioning, heat therapy if appropriate
- [ ] Report any changes in pain patterns, new pain, or concerns to the supervising clinician
- [ ] Document the effectiveness of non-pharmacological strategies used during the shift
- [ ] Communicate the pain management plan to the next caregiver

## 7. Special Considerations

### 7.1 Chronic Pain in Patients with Dementia
- Pain is vastly underrecognized and undertreated in people with dementia
- Behavioral changes (agitation, resistance to care, aggression, withdrawal, crying, moaning) may be the primary indicator of pain
- Use behavioral assessment tools (PAINAD Scale) consistently
- Non-pharmacological strategies are particularly important in this population because of the higher risk of medication side effects
- Familiar routines, gentle touch, music from the patient's past, and calm environments are especially effective
- Report ALL behavioral changes that might indicate pain

### 7.2 Chronic Pain and Depression
- Chronic pain and depression frequently coexist and amplify each other
- Signs of depression: persistent sadness, loss of interest, withdrawal, changes in appetite/sleep, hopelessness, frequent crying, expressing feelings of being a burden
- Caregivers should watch for and report signs of depression
- Maintaining social connections, activity, and meaningful engagement are important for both pain and mood
- Professional counseling and possibly medication may be needed; report concerns to the clinician

### 7.3 Cultural Considerations
- Pain expression varies across cultures; some patients may underreport pain due to cultural beliefs about stoicism, fear of addiction, or desire not to be a burden
- Ask open-ended questions about comfort and function, not just pain
- Respect cultural and spiritual practices related to pain and healing
- Some patients may prefer traditional healing approaches alongside conventional management; report these preferences to the care team so they can be integrated safely

### 7.4 Caregiver Self-Care
- Caring for someone with chronic pain can be emotionally and physically draining
- Caregivers are at risk for burnout, compassion fatigue, and their own musculoskeletal pain from caregiving tasks
- Use proper body mechanics during all caregiving tasks
- Take regular breaks
- Seek support from other family members, respite care services, or caregiver support groups
- Acknowledge the emotional impact of witnessing someone's suffering
- Seek help if you experience signs of burnout (exhaustion, detachment, irritability, feeling overwhelmed)

## 8. Key Evidence-Based Principles Summary

### CDC 2022 Clinical Practice Guideline (Nonopioid Treatments)
- Non-pharmacological therapies (exercise, physical therapy, CBT, multimodal approaches) are recommended as first-line treatments for many chronic pain conditions
- These approaches should be tried before or alongside pharmacological treatment
- Evidence supports exercise therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy, and multimodal rehabilitation for chronic low back pain, fibromyalgia, and osteoarthritis

### AGS Recommendations for Persistent Pain in Older Persons
- All older patients with chronic pain should have access to non-pharmacological approaches
- Exercise programs (tailored to ability) are essential for chronic pain management
- Non-pharmacological strategies should be part of a comprehensive, individualized pain management plan
- Pain in older adults is frequently underassessed and undertreated
- Behavioral interventions are effective and underutilized

### NICE NG193 (Chronic Pain, 2021)
- Exercise and physical activity programs are recommended for chronic primary pain
- Psychological therapies (especially acceptance and commitment therapy, CBT) are recommended
- TENS may be considered for certain conditions
- Acupuncture may be considered for chronic primary pain
- Emphasis on self-management and patient education

### NCCIH Summary of Evidence for Non-Pharmacological Approaches
- Strong evidence for: exercise, yoga, tai chi, massage, spinal manipulation, CBT, mindfulness-based stress reduction
- Moderate evidence for: acupuncture, relaxation techniques, heat therapy
- These approaches are generally safe when properly applied

## 9. Glossary of Terms for Caregivers

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Acute pain** | Short-term pain that results from tissue damage (injury, surgery); typically resolves as healing occurs |
| **Chronic pain** | Pain lasting more than 3 months; may or may not have an ongoing physical cause |
| **Activity pacing** | Balancing activity and rest to maintain consistent function without triggering pain flares |
| **Boom-bust cycle** | Pattern of overdoing on good days and then being unable to function on subsequent "bad" days |
| **CBT** | Cognitive Behavioral Therapy -- a psychological approach that helps change thoughts and behaviors related to pain |
| **Cryotherapy** | Use of cold for therapeutic purposes |
| **Diaphragmatic breathing** | Deep breathing using the diaphragm rather than shallow chest breathing; activates relaxation response |
| **Endorphins** | Natural chemicals produced by the body that reduce pain perception and improve mood |
| **Flare** | A temporary increase in pain intensity above the usual baseline level |
| **Guided imagery** | A relaxation technique using mental visualization of peaceful, pleasant scenes |
| **Neuropathic pain** | Pain caused by damage to or dysfunction of the nervous system (burning, shooting, tingling quality) |
| **Nociceptive pain** | Pain caused by tissue damage or inflammation (aching, throbbing quality) |
| **NRS** | Numeric Rating Scale -- 0-10 scale for patient-reported pain intensity |
| **PAINAD** | Pain Assessment in Advanced Dementia Scale -- behavioral observation tool for non-verbal patients |
| **PMR** | Progressive Muscle Relaxation -- systematic tension and release of muscle groups |
| **ROM** | Range of Motion -- the degree of movement available at a joint |
| **TENS** | Transcutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation -- a device that delivers mild electrical impulses to reduce pain |
| **Thermotherapy** | Use of heat for therapeutic purposes |
