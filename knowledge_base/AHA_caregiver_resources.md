## 2. Resources for Caregivers

- **URL:** https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/caregiver-support/resources-for-caregivers
- **Page Title:** Resources for Caregivers | American Heart Association

### Overview

This page consolidates practical tools and guidance for caregivers of people with cardiovascular disease and stroke. It addresses both the practical tasks of caregiving and the emotional dimensions of the role.

### Key Guidance Areas

#### 2.1 Understanding Your Role as a Caregiver

- A caregiver may be a spouse, adult child, sibling, friend, or neighbor
- Caregiving responsibilities may range from occasional help to round-the-clock care
- The caregiving role often develops gradually and can be difficult to recognize at first
- AHA encourages caregivers to formally identify themselves as caregivers to access available support

#### 2.2 Working with the Healthcare Team

- **Attend medical appointments:** Caregivers should accompany the patient when possible, bring a list of questions, take notes, and request written instructions
- **Understand the care plan:** Know the diagnosis, treatment goals, medications, dietary restrictions, activity limitations, and follow-up schedule
- **Communicate changes:** Report any changes in symptoms, behavior, or functional ability to the healthcare team promptly
- **Coordinate among providers:** When multiple doctors are involved, help ensure that all providers are aware of the full medication list and treatment plan

#### 2.3 Practical Caregiving Tasks

AHA highlights the following practical areas for cardiovascular caregivers:

- **Medication management:** Organizing medications, understanding what each is for, monitoring for side effects, ensuring adherence, and coordinating refills
- **Dietary management:** Understanding and preparing heart-healthy or condition-specific diets (low-sodium for heart failure, healthy balanced diet for stroke recovery)
- **Monitoring vitals and symptoms:** Taking blood pressure, monitoring weight daily (for heart failure), tracking symptoms, and keeping logs for healthcare providers
- **Activity support:** Encouraging appropriate physical activity, supporting cardiac rehabilitation attendance, balancing rest and activity
- **Transportation:** Getting the patient to appointments, rehabilitation, pharmacy, and social activities
- **Emotional support:** Providing companionship, encouragement, and patience through recovery and chronic disease management

#### 2.4 Caregiver Self-Care

AHA explicitly addresses the caregiver's own health needs:

- **Caregiver stress is a cardiovascular risk factor:** Chronic caregiving stress has been associated with increased risk of heart disease in the caregiver
- **Physical health:** Maintain your own medical appointments, exercise, eat well, and get adequate sleep
- **Emotional health:** Recognize signs of depression, anxiety, and burnout; seek counseling or support groups when needed
- **Social connections:** Avoid isolation; maintain relationships outside the caregiving role
- **Respite:** Take regular breaks from caregiving; use family, friends, adult day programs, or professional respite services
- **Ask for help:** Develop a list of specific tasks others can help with; accepting help is not a sign of weakness

#### 2.5 Financial and Insurance Navigation

- Understanding Medicare, Medicaid, and private insurance coverage for cardiac and stroke care
- Cardiac rehabilitation coverage and authorization
- Medication assistance programs
- Leave policies (FMLA awareness for working caregivers)
- Financial planning for chronic illness management

### Key Action Items for Caregivers (AHA Recommendations)

1. Learn about the specific heart or stroke condition and its management requirements
2. Keep an organized medication list and bring it to every appointment
3. Monitor daily weight (for heart failure patients) and blood pressure as directed
4. Learn the warning signs of heart attack, stroke, and heart failure decompensation
5. Know when to call 911 versus when to call the doctor
6. Prepare heart-healthy meals following dietary guidelines from the care team
7. Encourage and support cardiac rehabilitation and physical activity
8. Take care of your own physical and mental health
9. Build a support network and accept help from others
10. Keep important documents organized (insurance cards, medication lists, advance directives, emergency contacts)

## 3. Heart Failure Management for Caregivers

- **URL:** https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/heart-failure
- **Related Caregiver URL:** https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/heart-failure/living-with-heart-failure
- **Page Title:** Heart Failure | American Heart Association

### Overview

Heart failure (HF) is a chronic condition in which the heart cannot pump blood effectively enough to meet the body's needs. AHA provides extensive resources for patients and caregivers on managing HF at home, including daily monitoring, dietary changes, medication adherence, and recognizing warning signs that require immediate medical attention.

### 3.1 Understanding Heart Failure

- Heart failure does not mean the heart has stopped; it means the heart is not pumping as well as it should
- Types: Systolic (reduced ejection fraction, HFrEF) vs. diastolic (preserved ejection fraction, HFpEF); left-sided vs. right-sided
- Common causes: Coronary artery disease, high blood pressure, previous heart attack, valve disease, cardiomyopathy
- Heart failure is typically a progressive condition but can be managed with proper treatment and lifestyle modifications
- The goal of management is to slow progression, control symptoms, reduce hospitalizations, and maintain quality of life

### 3.2 Daily Monitoring -- Critical Caregiver Tasks

AHA emphasizes daily monitoring as one of the most important caregiver responsibilities in heart failure:

#### Daily Weight Monitoring
- **Weigh the patient at the same time every day** (ideally first thing in the morning, after urinating, before eating, in similar clothing)
- **Use the same scale** each time; place it on a hard, flat surface
- **Record the weight** in a log or tracking app
- **ESCALATION TRIGGER:** A sudden weight gain of 2-3 pounds in one day, or 5 pounds in one week, may indicate fluid retention and should be reported to the healthcare provider immediately
- Weight gain in heart failure often reflects fluid accumulation, not fat

#### Symptom Monitoring
- **Shortness of breath:** Track whether it occurs at rest, with activity, or when lying flat (orthopnea); note if it wakes the patient at night (paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea)
- **Edema (swelling):** Check ankles, feet, legs, and abdomen daily for swelling; note whether socks or shoes leave marks
- **Fatigue and weakness:** Monitor energy levels and ability to perform daily activities
- **Cough:** A persistent dry cough, especially when lying down, may be a heart failure symptom
- **Dizziness or lightheadedness:** May indicate medication effects or worsening condition
- **Confusion or impaired thinking:** Can be a sign of reduced cardiac output

#### Blood Pressure and Heart Rate
- Monitor blood pressure as directed by the care team
- Note any significant changes or readings outside the target range
- Track heart rate and report irregular rhythms

### 3.3 Dietary Management for Heart Failure

AHA dietary guidance is central to heart failure management:

#### Sodium Restriction
- Most HF patients are advised to limit sodium intake (commonly to 1,500-2,000 mg per day, though individual targets vary)
- **Reading food labels:** Learn to identify sodium content on nutrition labels; beware of hidden sodium in processed, canned, and restaurant foods
- **Cooking strategies:** Use herbs, spices, lemon, vinegar, and salt-free seasoning blends instead of salt; cook from scratch when possible; rinse canned foods
- **High-sodium foods to avoid or limit:** Processed meats (deli meats, bacon, sausage), canned soups, frozen meals, soy sauce, pickled foods, cheese, salted snacks, fast food
- **Dining out:** Request meals prepared without added salt; ask for sauces and dressings on the side

#### Fluid Management
- Some HF patients need to restrict fluid intake (typically 1.5-2 liters per day, as directed)
- All liquids count: water, coffee, tea, juice, soup, ice cream, gelatin
- Tips: Use smaller cups, spread fluid intake throughout the day, suck on ice chips or frozen fruit to manage thirst, track intake

#### Heart-Healthy Eating Pattern
- Emphasize fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins, and healthy fats
- Limit saturated and trans fats
- Limit added sugars
- Maintain a healthy weight
- Follow a Mediterranean or DASH-style eating pattern when appropriate

### 3.4 Medication Management for Heart Failure

Heart failure typically requires multiple medications. Caregiver responsibilities include:

- **Understanding each medication's purpose:** Common HF medications include ACE inhibitors/ARBs/ARNI, beta-blockers, diuretics, aldosterone antagonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, hydralazine/nitrates, and digoxin
- **Ensuring adherence:** Use pill organizers, set alarms/reminders, maintain a consistent schedule
- **Monitoring for side effects:**
  - Diuretics: excessive urination, dizziness, electrolyte imbalances (muscle cramps, weakness)
  - ACE inhibitors: persistent cough, dizziness, elevated potassium
  - Beta-blockers: fatigue, slow heart rate, cold extremities, dizziness
  - Digoxin: nausea, vision changes, confusion (toxicity signs)
- **Never skip or adjust medications** without consulting the healthcare provider
- **Refill management:** Ensure medications are refilled before they run out; maintain a current medication list

### 3.5 Activity and Exercise in Heart Failure

- **Cardiac rehabilitation:** AHA strongly recommends cardiac rehab for eligible HF patients; caregivers can support attendance and exercise continuation at home
- **Activity pacing:** Balance activity and rest throughout the day; encourage activity but respect the patient's limits
- **Energy conservation techniques:** Sit during activities when possible, use assistive devices, plan activities during times of higher energy, break tasks into smaller steps
- **Warning signs during activity:** Stop activity and rest if the patient experiences increased shortness of breath, chest pain, dizziness, or extreme fatigue; report these to the healthcare team

### 3.6 Emotional and Social Support in Heart Failure

- Depression is common in HF patients (estimated 20-40%) and may worsen outcomes
- Caregivers should watch for signs of depression: withdrawal, hopelessness, changes in appetite or sleep, lack of interest in activities
- Encourage social engagement and meaningful activities within the patient's capabilities
- AHA recommends professional counseling or support groups for both patient and caregiver
- Address quality-of-life concerns openly with the healthcare team
- Advance care planning discussions are important, especially for advanced heart failure

### 3.7 Heart Failure Warning Signs -- When to Act

AHA provides clear escalation guidance for caregivers:

#### Call 911 Immediately
- Severe shortness of breath or inability to breathe
- Chest pain or pressure that does not resolve with rest
- Fainting or loss of consciousness
- Coughing up pink, frothy sputum
- Rapid or irregular heartbeat with dizziness or fainting

#### Call the Doctor/Nurse Same Day
- Weight gain of 2-3 pounds in 24 hours or 5 pounds in a week
- Increased swelling in legs, ankles, feet, or abdomen
- Increased shortness of breath with usual activities or when lying flat
- New or worsening cough
- Feeling unusually tired or weak
- Decreased appetite or nausea
- Increased confusion or difficulty thinking
- Decreased urine output

#### Report at Next Visit
- Gradual changes in exercise tolerance
- Questions about medications or diet
- Mood or behavioral changes
- Sleep difficulties

## 4. Stroke Recovery and Caregiver Resources

- **URL:** https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/stroke
- **Related URL:** https://www.stroke.org/en/life-after-stroke (American Stroke Association)
- **Page Title:** Stroke | American Heart Association / American Stroke Association

### Overview

The American Stroke Association (a division of AHA) provides comprehensive resources for stroke survivors and their caregivers. Stroke recovery is highly variable; some survivors regain most function while others face long-term disabilities. Caregivers play a central role in supporting rehabilitation, managing daily care, preventing recurrent stroke, and addressing the emotional impact on both the survivor and themselves.

### 4.1 Understanding Stroke

- **Ischemic stroke:** Caused by a blood clot blocking a blood vessel in the brain (about 87% of strokes)
- **Hemorrhagic stroke:** Caused by a blood vessel bursting in the brain
- **Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA):** A temporary blockage sometimes called a mini-stroke; a TIA is a serious warning sign
- Effects of stroke depend on the location and extent of brain damage
- Common effects: Weakness or paralysis on one side of the body (hemiparesis/hemiplegia), speech and language difficulties (aphasia), vision problems, cognitive changes, emotional changes, swallowing difficulties (dysphagia), fatigue

### 4.2 Supporting Stroke Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation is the cornerstone of stroke recovery. The caregiver's role includes:

#### Encouraging Therapy Participation
- Support attendance at physical therapy (PT), occupational therapy (OT), and speech-language pathology (SLP) sessions
- Understand the therapy goals and exercises prescribed
- Encourage practice of therapeutic exercises at home between sessions
- Celebrate progress, no matter how small; recovery can be slow but ongoing

#### Physical Rehabilitation Support
- **Range of motion exercises:** Help the survivor perform prescribed range-of-motion exercises to prevent contractures (joint stiffness) on the affected side
- **Mobility assistance:** Help with walking, transfers, and wheelchair use; use proper body mechanics to prevent caregiver injury
- **Fall prevention:** Stroke survivors have high fall risk due to balance impairment, weakness, and spatial neglect; clear pathways, use assistive devices, ensure good lighting, remove trip hazards
- **Adaptive equipment:** Reachers, long-handled sponges, button hooks, rocker knives, non-slip mats, built-up handles on utensils, shower chairs, grab bars

#### ADL Adaptations for One-Sided Weakness
- **Dressing:** Teach the principle of dressing the affected side first and undressing it last; use front-opening garments, adaptive fasteners (Velcro, magnets, elastic laces)
- **Bathing:** Use a shower chair, handheld showerhead, and long-handled sponge; install grab bars; ensure non-slip surfaces
- **Eating:** Position the plate within the visual field (especially important for visual neglect); use adaptive utensils, plate guards, and non-slip mats; assist with cutting food if needed
- **Grooming:** Set up supplies on the unaffected side; use electric razors for safety; suction cup devices can stabilize items for one-handed use

### 4.3 Communication After Stroke (Aphasia and Dysarthria)

Communication difficulties are common after stroke and can be profoundly frustrating for both the survivor and caregiver:

#### Types of Communication Problems
- **Aphasia:** Difficulty with language (understanding words, finding words, reading, writing); caused by damage to language areas of the brain
  - **Expressive aphasia (Broca's):** The person knows what they want to say but has difficulty getting the words out; speech may be slow, halting, and effortful
  - **Receptive aphasia (Wernicke's):** The person has difficulty understanding spoken or written language; speech may be fluent but not make sense
  - **Global aphasia:** Severe impairment in both understanding and producing language
- **Dysarthria:** Difficulty with the physical production of speech (slurred, slow, or quiet speech) due to weakness of the muscles used for speaking; language comprehension is typically intact
- **Apraxia of speech:** Difficulty coordinating the muscle movements needed for speech; the person knows what to say but has trouble programming the movements

#### Communication Strategies for Caregivers
- **Be patient:** Allow extra time for the person to communicate; do not rush or complete their sentences unless they signal for help
- **Use simple language:** Short sentences and simple words; speak at a normal pace (not overly slow or loud)
- **Ask yes/no questions** when possible to reduce frustration
- **Use visual aids:** Pictures, gestures, pointing to objects, writing, communication boards, and tablet apps
- **Reduce background noise:** Turn off the TV or radio during conversations
- **Maintain eye contact** and face the person directly when speaking
- **Confirm understanding:** Repeat back what you think the person is saying to confirm
- **Encourage any form of communication:** Gestures, drawing, pointing, and writing are all valid
- **Do not pretend to understand** if you do not; ask the person to try again in a different way
- **Treat the person as an adult:** Aphasia does not mean the person is intellectually impaired; do not talk down to them or talk about them in their presence as if they cannot understand

### 4.4 Swallowing Safety After Stroke (Dysphagia)

Dysphagia is a common and serious complication of stroke:

#### Understanding the Risk
- About 50% of stroke survivors initially experience swallowing difficulties
- Dysphagia increases the risk of aspiration (food or liquid entering the airway), which can lead to aspiration pneumonia
- A speech-language pathologist (SLP) should evaluate swallowing ability; follow their recommendations precisely

#### Caregiver Responsibilities for Safe Swallowing
- **Follow prescribed diet textures:** The SLP may recommend thickened liquids or texture-modified foods (pureed, minced, soft); use the prescribed thickening agent at the correct consistency
- **Positioning during meals:** The person should sit upright (90 degrees) during eating and for at least 30 minutes after a meal
- **Supervise meals:** Watch for signs of swallowing difficulty (coughing, choking, wet or gurgly voice quality, food pocketing in the cheek, watery eyes, throat clearing)
- **Small bites and sips:** Encourage small amounts and allow adequate time for chewing and swallowing before the next bite
- **Minimize distractions:** Eating should occur in a calm, focused environment
- **Oral care:** Good oral hygiene before and after meals reduces the bacterial load and the risk of aspiration pneumonia; brush teeth, clean dentures, and use mouthwash as appropriate

#### ESCALATION: Signs of Aspiration
- Coughing or choking during or after eating/drinking
- Wet, gurgly, or hoarse voice quality during or after meals
- Recurring pneumonia or respiratory infections
- Unexplained fever
- Feeling of food "sticking" in the throat
- **Action:** Report any of these signs to the healthcare provider immediately; do not change the diet texture without SLP guidance

### 4.5 Preventing Recurrent Stroke

Approximately 25% of strokes are recurrent. Caregivers play a key role in secondary prevention:

#### Modifiable Risk Factor Management
- **Blood pressure control:** Monitor BP as directed; support medication adherence and lifestyle modifications (sodium reduction, exercise, stress management, limiting alcohol)
- **Cholesterol management:** Support a heart-healthy diet and statin medication adherence if prescribed
- **Blood sugar control:** If the patient has diabetes, support glucose monitoring and dietary management
- **Anticoagulation/antiplatelet therapy:** Ensure adherence to prescribed blood thinners (warfarin, DOACs) or antiplatelet agents (aspirin, clopidogrel); be aware of bleeding risks
- **Smoking cessation:** Support quitting if the patient smokes; avoid secondhand smoke exposure
- **Physical activity:** Encourage regular activity as tolerated and approved by the healthcare team
- **Weight management:** Support a healthy weight through diet and activity
- **Atrial fibrillation management:** If the patient has AFib, ensure medication adherence and attend follow-up appointments

#### Medication Adherence for Secondary Prevention
- Create a medication schedule and use organizers
- Understand the purpose of each medication (e.g., blood thinner to prevent clots, statin for cholesterol, antihypertensive for blood pressure)
- Know the signs of medication complications (e.g., unusual bleeding or bruising with blood thinners)
- Never discontinue medications without medical guidance

### 4.6 Emotional Recovery After Stroke

The emotional impact of stroke on both survivor and caregiver is significant:

#### Post-Stroke Depression and Emotional Changes
- Depression affects approximately 33% of stroke survivors
- Emotional lability (pseudobulbar affect) -- sudden, uncontrollable laughing or crying -- is a neurological effect of stroke, not a psychological response
- Anxiety, frustration, anger, and grief are common after stroke
- Personality changes may occur depending on the area of brain affected
- Apathy and lack of motivation can be symptoms of the stroke, not laziness

#### Caregiver Guidance for Emotional Support
- Validate the survivor's feelings; do not dismiss frustration or sadness
- Encourage engagement in enjoyable activities and social interactions
- Be aware of the signs of depression and report them to the healthcare team
- Encourage professional counseling or support groups
- Understand that emotional lability is a medical condition that may improve with treatment
- Be patient with behavioral and personality changes; consult the healthcare team

#### Caregiver Emotional Health
- Stroke caregiver burnout is well-documented in the research literature
- Caregivers frequently experience depression, anxiety, fatigue, and social isolation
- The sudden onset of stroke (unlike the gradual progression of dementia) can cause acute adjustment difficulties for caregivers
- Role changes within the relationship can be difficult (e.g., a spouse becoming a full-time caregiver)
- AHA/ASA recommends caregivers: seek their own support network, join caregiver support groups, maintain their own health, take regular breaks, accept help

### 4.7 Stroke Warning Signs -- FAST and Beyond

AHA/ASA urges all caregivers and family members to know the signs of stroke:

#### FAST Acronym
- **F -- Face drooping:** Ask the person to smile. Does one side of the face droop?
- **A -- Arm weakness:** Ask the person to raise both arms. Does one arm drift downward?
- **S -- Speech difficulty:** Ask the person to repeat a simple phrase. Is their speech slurred or strange?
- **T -- Time to call 911:** If you observe any of these signs, call 911 immediately. Note the time when symptoms first appeared.

#### Additional Stroke Warning Signs
- Sudden numbness or weakness of the face, arm, or leg, especially on one side of the body
- Sudden confusion or trouble understanding speech
- Sudden trouble seeing in one or both eyes
- Sudden trouble walking, dizziness, or loss of balance or coordination
- Sudden severe headache with no known cause

#### Critical Caregiver Action
- **Call 911 immediately** -- do not drive the patient to the hospital yourself unless no ambulance is available
- **Note the exact time** symptoms started or were first noticed; this affects treatment eligibility (e.g., tPA administration window)
- **Do not give food, drink, or medication** until the patient has been evaluated
- Stroke treatment is time-sensitive; faster treatment leads to better outcomes

## 5. Emergency Recognition

### 5.1 Heart Attack Warning Signs

- Chest discomfort or pain (pressure, squeezing, fullness, or pain in the center of the chest; may come and go)
- Discomfort in other areas of the upper body (one or both arms, back, neck, jaw, or stomach)
- Shortness of breath (with or without chest discomfort)
- Other signs: cold sweat, nausea, lightheadedness
- **Women may be more likely** to experience shortness of breath, nausea/vomiting, and back or jaw pain
- **Action:** Call 911 immediately. Do not wait to see if symptoms go away. Have the patient chew an aspirin (unless they are allergic or have been told not to) while waiting for emergency services.

### 5.2 Cardiac Arrest vs. Heart Attack

- **Heart attack:** Blood flow to the heart is blocked; the person is typically conscious
- **Cardiac arrest:** The heart stops beating effectively; the person becomes unresponsive and stops breathing normally
- **Action for cardiac arrest:** Call 911 immediately, begin CPR (push hard and fast on the center of the chest), use an AED (automated external defibrillator) if one is available
- AHA encourages caregivers and family members to take a CPR training course
