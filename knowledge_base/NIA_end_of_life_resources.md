## 2. Understanding Hospice and Palliative Care

### Palliative Care

**Definition:** Palliative care is specialized medical care for people living with serious illness. It focuses on providing relief from symptoms, pain, and stress -- regardless of the diagnosis. The goal is to improve quality of life for both the patient and the family.

**Key Characteristics:**
- Available at any age and any stage of a serious illness
- Can be provided alongside curative or disease-modifying treatments
- Delivered by a specially trained interdisciplinary team
- Addresses physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs
- Focuses on goals of care, communication, and shared decision-making
- Does NOT require stopping treatment for the underlying disease
- Available in hospitals, outpatient clinics, long-term care facilities, and at home

**Common Conditions Served:**
- Cancer
- Heart failure (CHF)
- Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
- Kidney disease / end-stage renal disease
- Dementia / Alzheimer's disease
- Parkinson's disease
- Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)
- Stroke
- Liver disease

**How to Access:**
- Ask the primary care provider or specialist for a palliative care referral
- Search the Center to Advance Palliative Care (CAPC) provider directory: https://getpalliativecare.org/
- Palliative care is covered by most insurance plans, Medicare, and Medicaid

### Hospice Care

**Definition:** Hospice care is a type of palliative care specifically for people who are nearing the end of life (generally with a life expectancy of six months or less if the disease runs its normal course). Hospice focuses entirely on comfort and quality of life rather than on curing the illness.

**Key Characteristics:**
- Requires a physician certification that the patient has a terminal prognosis (six months or less)
- Curative treatments are generally discontinued; focus shifts to comfort
- Patient can revoke hospice and return to curative treatment at any time
- Provided wherever the patient lives (home, nursing facility, assisted living, hospice inpatient facility)
- Interdisciplinary team: physician, nurse, home health aide, social worker, chaplain, volunteers, bereavement counselor
- Includes medications, medical equipment, and supplies related to the terminal diagnosis
- 24/7 on-call nursing support
- Respite care for family caregivers (up to five consecutive days in an inpatient setting)
- Bereavement support for family for 13 months after death

**Medicare Hospice Benefit:**
- Part A (hospital insurance) covers hospice services
- Patient pays little to nothing for hospice care
- Covers: nursing care, medical social services, physician services, counseling, home health aide and homemaker services, short-term inpatient and respite care, medications for symptom management, durable medical equipment (hospital bed, wheelchair, walker), supplies (bandages, catheters), and bereavement support
- Patient may still see their primary care physician
- Patient pays no more than a $5 copay for each prescription for outpatient drugs for pain and symptom management
- Patient pays 5% of the Medicare-approved amount for inpatient respite care

**How to Access:**
- Talk to the patient's physician about hospice eligibility
- Contact local hospice programs directly
- Use the NHPCO hospice locator: https://www.nhpco.org/find-a-provider/
- Medicare Hospice Compare: https://www.medicare.gov/care-compare/

### Palliative Care vs. Hospice Care -- Key Differences

| Feature | Palliative Care | Hospice Care |
|---------|----------------|--------------|
| Timing | Any stage of serious illness | Terminal prognosis (6 months or less) |
| Curative treatment | Can continue alongside | Generally discontinued |
| Goal | Improve quality of life; symptom relief | Comfort and dignity at end of life |
| Setting | Hospital, clinic, home, facility | Home, facility, hospice center |
| Insurance | Most plans, Medicare, Medicaid | Medicare Part A, Medicaid, most insurers |
| Duration | No time limit | Recertified in benefit periods |
| Requirement | Serious illness diagnosis | Physician certification of terminal prognosis |

## 3. Comfort Care at Home

### Overview

Comfort care (also called supportive care) focuses on managing symptoms and maintaining quality of life. When a person is receiving end-of-life care at home, family caregivers play a central role in providing day-to-day comfort measures.

### Pain Management

- **Assessing pain:** Use a consistent method to assess pain (0-10 scale, FACES scale for nonverbal patients, behavioral cues such as grimacing, moaning, restlessness, guarding)
- **Medication management:** Administer pain medications on a regular schedule rather than only when pain becomes severe; understand the difference between long-acting and breakthrough pain medications
- **Types of pain medications commonly used:**
  - Non-opioid analgesics (acetaminophen, NSAIDs)
  - Opioids (morphine, oxycodone, hydromorphone, fentanyl) -- cornerstone of end-of-life pain management
  - Adjuvant medications (gabapentin for nerve pain, corticosteroids for inflammation, muscle relaxants)
- **Non-pharmacological comfort measures:**
  - Repositioning (every 2 hours or as needed)
  - Gentle massage
  - Heat or cold application
  - Music therapy, guided imagery, relaxation techniques
  - Distraction and companionship
- **Key caregiver guidance:**
  - Do not be afraid of pain medications; when used as directed, they are safe and effective
  - Report uncontrolled pain to the hospice team immediately
  - Keep a pain diary to track patterns and medication effectiveness
  - Understand that increasing pain medication needs are normal as illness progresses, not a sign of addiction

### Breathing and Respiratory Comfort

- **Dyspnea (shortness of breath):** Common symptom in end-of-life care
  - Position the person upright or in a position of comfort (often semi-Fowler's position with head elevated)
  - Use a fan directed at the face (air movement provides relief)
  - Administer supplemental oxygen if prescribed
  - Medications: opioids (low dose) can relieve the sensation of breathlessness; anti-anxiety medications may help
  - Open windows for fresh air when possible
  - Create a calm, uncluttered environment
- **Noisy breathing / "death rattle":** Caused by secretions in the throat that the person can no longer clear
  - Often sounds worse to the caregiver than it feels to the patient
  - Reposition the person on their side to allow secretions to drain
  - Gentle oral suctioning (avoid deep suctioning, which is uncomfortable)
  - Medications (scopolamine patch, glycopyrrolate, atropine drops) can reduce secretions
  - Reassure family that this is a normal part of the dying process

### Skin and Mouth Care

- **Skin care:**
  - Reposition regularly to prevent pressure injuries
  - Keep skin clean and dry; use gentle cleansers
  - Apply moisturizers to prevent dryness and cracking
  - Check bony prominences daily (sacrum, heels, elbows, shoulder blades)
  - Use pressure-redistribution surfaces (specialty mattress overlays, heel protectors)
  - At end of life, skin may become more fragile and bruise easily; handle with extreme gentleness
  - Kennedy terminal ulcer: a specific type of pressure injury that can appear rapidly (usually on the sacrum) as a sign that death is near; this is not a result of poor care
- **Mouth care:**
  - Dry mouth is very common at end of life
  - Use mouth swabs (toothettes) moistened with water to keep the mouth moist
  - Apply lip balm or petroleum jelly to prevent cracking
  - Small ice chips or sips of water if the person can still swallow safely
  - Avoid glycerin or lemon swabs (can further dry the mouth)
  - Mouth care should be performed regularly, at least every 2-4 hours

### Nutrition and Hydration at End of Life

- **Decreased appetite and thirst:** Normal and expected as the body naturally begins to shut down
- **Key guidance for caregivers:**
  - Do not force food or fluids; the body's decreasing need for nutrition is a natural part of the dying process
  - Offer small amounts of favorite foods or fluids if the person desires them
  - Ice chips, popsicles, and sips of water may be comforting
  - Artificial nutrition (tube feeding) is generally not recommended in end-of-life care; it does not prolong life or improve comfort and carries risks (aspiration, infection, discomfort)
  - IV fluids may contribute to uncomfortable fluid overload (edema, pulmonary congestion)
  - Mouth care becomes the primary comfort measure when the person is no longer eating or drinking
  - Reassure family members that not eating is not causing the person to suffer
- **Cultural sensitivity:** Food is deeply connected to caregiving across cultures; provide emotional support to family members who struggle with the idea of their loved one not eating

### Temperature and Environmental Comfort

- Keep the room at a comfortable temperature
- Use light blankets (the person may alternate between feeling hot and cold)
- Remove heavy bedding if the person appears overheated
- Use cool cloths on the forehead for comfort
- Maintain a quiet, peaceful environment
- Minimize bright overhead lighting; use soft, natural light
- Allow familiar sounds, music, or voices

### Emotional and Psychological Comfort

- **Presence:** Being present is one of the most important things a caregiver can do; sitting quietly, holding hands, speaking softly
- **Reassurance:** Remind the person that they are loved, safe, and not alone
- **Active listening:** Allow the person to express fears, regrets, or wishes without judgment
- **Permission to let go:** Some dying people seem to wait for permission from loved ones; gently telling them it is okay to let go can provide peace
- **Life review:** Encouraging the person to share memories and stories can be meaningful and therapeutic
- **Privacy and dignity:** Respect the person's need for privacy during personal care; maintain dignity at all times

### Spiritual and Cultural Care

- Respect the person's spiritual beliefs, religious traditions, and cultural practices
- Arrange for visits from clergy, spiritual advisors, or community leaders if desired
- Support rituals, prayers, or practices that are meaningful to the person and family
- Hospice chaplains are available for spiritual support regardless of religious affiliation
- Address spiritual distress (fear, guilt, questions about meaning) with compassion and referral to appropriate support

## 4. Understanding the Dying Process

### Signs That Death May Be Approaching (Weeks to Days Before Death)

**Physical Changes:**
- Increasing sleepiness and fatigue; sleeping much of the day
- Decreased appetite and thirst; reduced or no interest in food or fluids
- Withdrawal from social interaction; decreased interest in surroundings
- Increased confusion, disorientation, or restlessness (terminal restlessness/agitation)
- Changes in breathing patterns (Cheyne-Stokes respiration: cycles of deep breathing followed by periods of no breathing)
- Increased pain or discomfort in some patients
- Progressive weakness and inability to get out of bed
- Difficulty swallowing medications; may need to switch to liquid, sublingual, or rectal formulations
- Changes in urine output (decreased, darker, or concentrated)
- Cool, mottled, or bluish skin, especially on extremities (mottling)
- Swelling (edema) in extremities

**Emotional and Behavioral Changes:**
- May appear to "rally" with a brief period of increased alertness and energy before death
- May report seeing deceased loved ones or describe visions (nearing death awareness)
- May become more introspective or withdrawn
- May express a desire to resolve unfinished business or say goodbye

### Signs in the Final Hours

- Breathing becomes irregular, with long pauses (apneic episodes)
- Noisy, gurgling breathing (death rattle) caused by pooling secretions
- Skin becomes cool, pale, or mottled; nail beds and lips may turn bluish (cyanosis)
- Extremities may feel cold to the touch
- Eyes may remain partially open, unfocused, or glazed
- Jaw may relax and mouth may fall open
- No response to verbal or physical stimulation
- Pulse becomes weak, rapid, or irregular; blood pressure drops
- Urine output stops or becomes very minimal

### What Caregivers Can Do During the Final Hours

- Continue to speak softly and reassuringly; hearing is believed to be the last sense to diminish
- Hold the person's hand, stroke their arm, or provide gentle touch
- Keep the room quiet and peaceful
- Continue mouth care and lip moistening
- Reposition gently for comfort
- Allow family members to be present if they wish
- Play soft music or read aloud if it seems comforting
- Do not try to wake the person or force interaction
- Contact the hospice team with any concerns or questions

### Immediately After Death

- There is no emergency; take as much time as needed
- If under hospice care, call the hospice program (not 911); a hospice nurse will come to the home to confirm death and provide guidance
- If NOT under hospice care, follow local protocols (may need to call 911 or the physician)
- Allow family time to be with the body, say goodbye, and perform any religious or cultural rituals
- The hospice team will guide the family through next steps (contacting a funeral home, managing medications, returning medical equipment)
- Unused controlled medications (opioids) should be disposed of properly; the hospice nurse will guide this process

## 5. Advance Directives and Planning Ahead

### Types of Advance Directives

| Document | Purpose | Key Details |
|----------|---------|-------------|
| Living Will | Documents preferences for medical treatment at end of life | Specifies wishes about CPR, ventilators, tube feeding, dialysis, antibiotics, comfort care |
| Durable Power of Attorney for Health Care (Health Care Proxy) | Designates a person to make medical decisions if you cannot | Agent should understand and be willing to honor the person's wishes |
| POLST (Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment) | Medical orders that translate patient preferences into actionable physician orders | Also known as MOLST, MOST, or POST depending on the state; must be signed by a physician |
| DNR (Do Not Resuscitate) Order | Instructs healthcare providers not to perform CPR | Can be part of advance directives or a standalone medical order |

### Advance Care Planning Conversations

**When to have the conversation:**
- Ideally, before a health crisis; can be initiated at any time
- At diagnosis of a serious or chronic illness
- After a hospitalization or health setback
- During routine medical visits
- When updating estate planning or legal documents

**How to start the conversation:**
- Choose a calm, private time
- Express your own wishes as a way to open dialogue
- Ask open-ended questions about values and goals (rather than specific medical procedures)
- Use conversation guides such as The Conversation Project (theconversationproject.org), Five Wishes (fivewishes.org), or PREPARE for Your Care (prepareforyourcare.org)
- Focus on what matters most to the person: quality of life, being free from pain, being surrounded by loved ones, maintaining independence, following spiritual or cultural practices

**Key Questions to Explore:**
- What does a good day look like for you?
- What are you most afraid of regarding your health?
- If you could not speak for yourself, who would you trust to make decisions for you?
- Under what conditions would you not want life-sustaining treatment?
- How important is it to be at home versus in a hospital?
- Are there specific religious or cultural practices that should be observed?
- What would you want your family to know about your wishes?

### Sharing and Updating Advance Directives

- Give copies to the healthcare proxy, primary care provider, specialists, and close family members
- Keep the original in an accessible location (NOT a safe deposit box)
- Review and update regularly, especially after a major health event, change in marital status, or change in preferences
- Register with state registries where available
- Bring copies to every hospital admission

## 6. Supporting Families and Emotional Care

### Anticipatory Grief

- Grief that occurs before the actual death, as family members begin to process the impending loss
- Common feelings: sadness, anger, guilt, anxiety, helplessness, ambivalence, yearning
- Anticipatory grief can actually be beneficial, allowing families to prepare emotionally, resolve conflicts, and say goodbye
- Normalize these feelings; they are a natural response to watching a loved one decline
- Encourage family members to express their feelings and seek support

### Caregiver Emotional Needs at End of Life

- **Exhaustion and burnout:** End-of-life caregiving can be physically and emotionally draining; encourage caregivers to accept help and take breaks
- **Guilt:** Caregivers may feel guilty about wanting relief, about decisions made, or about perceived inadequacy
- **Fear:** Fear of doing something wrong, fear of being present at the moment of death, fear of not being present
- **Isolation:** Caregivers may withdraw from their social network; encourage maintaining connections
- **Complicated emotions:** It is possible to feel relief alongside grief; this is normal and should not cause additional guilt

### Supporting Children and Adolescents

- Use age-appropriate, honest language about what is happening
- Allow children to visit if they wish, but prepare them for what they will see
- Reassure children that the illness is not their fault
- Maintain routines as much as possible
- Be available to answer questions and provide comfort
- Consider grief counseling for children if needed

### Bereavement Support

- Grief after death is individual; there is no right way or timeline
- Normal grief reactions include sadness, crying, anger, difficulty concentrating, sleep disruption, appetite changes, physical symptoms (fatigue, headaches)
- Complicated grief (prolonged grief disorder) may require professional support -- signs include inability to function months after the death, persistent yearning, difficulty accepting the reality of the loss
- Hospice programs provide bereavement support for 13 months after the death of the patient
- Additional resources: grief counselors, support groups, faith-based programs, online communities
